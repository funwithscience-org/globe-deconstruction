If a pendulum bob is launched with even a microscopic sideways
velocity, it does not swing in a flat, straight line; it swings in a narrow
ellipse. Non-linear physics dictates that an elliptical pendulum
precesses on its own (called Airy precession or area precession). This
can speed up, slow down, or completely counteract the Coriolis effect.

In 1879, Nobel laureate Heike Kamerlingh Onnes dedicated his doctoral
thesis to why Foucault pendulums frequently failed or produced
inconsistent results. He proved that if the mounting fixture or wire is
even slightly stiffer in one direction than another, the pendulum will
alter its oscillation pattern and deviate from expected rates.

Tiny temperature shifts cause the cable to expand unevenly, subtle air
drafts from ventilation systems push the bob, and ground vibrations
from passing traffic distort the path.

Because air drag eventually stops a pendulum, public exhibit

magnet is misaligned by even a fraction of a millimeter, it artificially
"steers" the pendulum, giving a false precession rate.
