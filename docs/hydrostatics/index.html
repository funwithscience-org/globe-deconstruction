    Bending the Laws of Hydrostatics

    Q2 of the Globe Deconstruction Review: what a 1,000-metre warehouse would actually show, why the number is 19.6 mm and not 3 inches, and why nobody has built it

    Unreviewed first draft. Published for review only, not linked as an answer in the catalogue, marked noindex.
    • Corrected after the checking pass: the error table was headed as a budget while listing each term at several magnitudes, so a reader summing it got 137% — it is now labelled a sensitivity table; 8 × (1000/1609.344)² was printed as 78.48 mm, which is d²/2R rather than the rule’s own 78.46, on the page teaching that two near quantities differ; the wire-sag densities were undisclosed; the outdoor refraction coefficients are now flagged as benchmarks that may not hold indoors and could invert in sign; a deflection-of-the-vertical figure was understated as “percent-level” when an arcsecond per kilometre is already 3%; and a dangling open question referenced a SLAC figure the page does not carry.
    Treat any figure as provisional until this notice is removed.

    Miller asks why no one has proved that gravity is strong enough to bend the laws of hydrostatics in a controlled environment, and proposes a temperature-controlled kilometre-long water channel that should show 3 inches of mid-span rise. The experiment is a good one. The 3 inches is a geometry slip we can correct using his own rule at half the distance: 8 × (500/1609.344)² = 0.772 in = 19.6 mm. But correcting the arithmetic does not answer him, because "level" is a property of the field rather than of water, and every classic liquid demonstration is equally consistent with a uniform downward field. The real crux is whether the local vertical converges — and the reason nobody builds the warehouse is that water at a kilometre is a manometer, a capillary, a seiche basin and a thermometer before it is a curvature detector.

    Where this lands

    Miller's formula is correct and correctly applied to the wrong two points; the answer is 19.6 mm, not 3 inches, recoverable from his own eight-inches-per-mile rule at half the span. His framing dissolves on inspection — hydrostatic equilibrium is defined relative to whatever body force applies, so nothing has to be overridden. But his evidential demand survives that dissolution intact: Newton's bucket, Plateau's drop, the Moses effect and diamagnetic levitation are all equally consistent with a parallel field, and none of them settle whether the local vertical converges. His literal claim that the experiment has never been performed appears to be true. Built properly — 50 mm wide, sealed gas headspace, deliberately damped, referenced to an evacuated optical line — it would work and would read 19.6 mm. Nobody does it because there is no reason to, not because there is a reason not to.

    The question, in his words

    Q2 verbatim: "Why has no one proved that spacetime curvature (known as gravity) is strong enough to literally bend the laws of hydrostatics in a controlled environment?"

    The experiment, verbatim, from the Hydrostatics & Optics chapter: "building a skinny warehouse that is 1000 meters long (3,281 feet). You would build a skinny container across the whole warehouse with temperature control. Then you could use precise measuring equipment to show that the water in the middle is 3 inches higher compared to the left and right edges."

    This page answers that question, not a paraphrase of it. The experiment is a good one. The number attached to it is off by a factor recoverable from Miller's own rule. And the reason nobody has built it is not the reason he supposes. One part of his complaint is still standing at the end, and we say where.

    First, the concession: water is flat

    At every scale at which a human being has actually looked at water, the globe model's own prediction is that water is flat — not flat-ish, but flat below the threshold of whatever instrument is doing the looking.

    SpanMid-span rise above the two ends, d²/8R
    1.5 m bathtub0.044 µm
    10 m backyard pool2.0 µm
    50 m Olympic pool49 µm
    100 m0.196 mm
    1,000 m (Miller's warehouse)19.62 mm

    A human hair is about 70 µm across. An Olympic pool's predicted curvature is smaller than that. A bathtub's is about a twelfth of a wavelength of green light. Anyone who says "look, water is flat" is saying something true and quantitatively defensible.

    Miller chose 1,000 m because it is the first span at which the number stops being absurd. The temperature control is the same instinct: thermal stratification is the classic confound in long liquid-level work, exactly what accelerator metrologists design around, and he named it inside a two-sentence design description. Most critiques of any model never name a length, an expected displacement, and a control at all.

    The arithmetic, using his own rule at half the distance

    Miller predicts 3 inches. The globe model predicts 0.77 inch. Seeing why needs no second formula — only his formula, applied over half the span.

    The eight-inches-per-mile-squared rule gives the drop of a sphere's surface below a tangent line grazing it at one end. Over 1,000 m: 8 × (1000/1609.344)² = 3.089 in = 78.46 mm. That is his 3 inches, and it is a correct application of a correct rule.

    But the quantity his sentence describes is a different one. "The water in the middle is 3 inches higher compared to the left and right edges" references the middle to both ends, not a tangent at one. That is the sagitta, and an identity connects the two without leaving his toolkit: `d²/8R = (d/2)²/2R`. The mid-span rise over 1,000 m is the eight-inches-per-mile drop computed over 500 m.

    8 × (500/1609.344)² = 0.772 in = 19.6 mm. Same rule he used. Half the distance. A quarter of the answer.

    That is the whole correction: the middle of a chord is a quarter of the way to its end, not the whole way. This is a geometry slip of the kind that appears constantly in the surveying literature itself, and the rule he reached for is the same rule LIGO's alignment document uses as a load-bearing input. He had the right tool. He pointed it at the wrong two points.

    The exact spherical forms at R = 6,371,000 m, no small-angle approximation: sagitta R − √(R² − (d/2)²) = 19.6201538522 mm; tangent drop √(R² + d²) − R = 78.4806148046 mm; ratio 3.999999969.

    The ratio is 4.0000 to five figures, and exactly 4 only in the small-angle limit where the expressions reduce to d²/8R and d²/2R — the exact forms deviate from 4 in opposite directions. Anyone quoting "exactly 4" of the exact spherical expressions is wrong at the digit the claim is being made to.

    "Level" is a property of the field, not of water

    The phrase "strong enough to bend the laws of hydrostatics" presumes a contest between two things. There is no contest, and Miller's own paragraph is the reason.

    He writes that water "cannot resist shear stress; its molecules slide freely until they exert a force perpendicular to the container walls, achieving hydrostatic equilibrium… distributing its mass until the internal pressure gradients are balanced." That is the textbook definition, and written out it is ∇p = ρg: the pressure gradient equals the applied body force per unit volume, whatever that body force happens to be. Hydrostatic equilibrium is not a rule a field must overpower. It is a statement about the field, with the field left free.

    The free surface is therefore the surface of constant pressure, everywhere perpendicular to the effective body force — gravity plus the centrifugal term of rotation, which is why the level surface is a spheroid and not a sphere. Water does not resist g and lose. Water reports g.

    So "is gravity strong enough to override hydrostatics?" has no target, and the question that does is: what shape is the field? Uniform and downward gives a plane. Convergent gives a sphere. Identical hydrostatics; different answer, because different field. That liquids really do take non-flat free surfaces whenever the field says so is not a device invented for this argument.

    Newton's rotating bucket (1687). Principia, Scholium to the Definitions: the water will "recede by little and little, and ascend to the sides of the vessel, forming itself into a concave figure." The surface is a paraboloid, z = ω²r²/2g. A 300 mm bucket at 78 rpm puts the rim 76 mm above the centre — four times Miller's kilometre-long signal, in a bucket, in a minute. (Strictly it is hydrostatic in the co-rotating frame; in the lab frame it is a steady rotating flow.) The Moses effect. Water is diamagnetic, χ ≈ −8.8 × 10⁻⁶; a field gradient exerts a body force on it and visibly parts the free surface. Ueno & Iwasaka, IEEE Transactions on Magnetics 30(6), 4698–4700 (1994). We could not retrieve the full text, so we quote no magnitude — the ones we can compute are idealised ceilings, not measurements. Berry & Geim's levitated frog (1997). European Journal of Physics 18, 307–313. At Nijmegen, at a predicted central field of 16.5 T, they levitated water droplets and a live frog, and give the condition for water to float: B(z)B′(z) = −1400.9 T² m⁻¹. That is hydrostatics with the net body force set to zero, in a room, on a Tuesday. Plateau's density-matched drop (from 1840). Olive oil in an alcohol–water mixture tuned to the oil's density, so buoyancy cancels weight; the drop becomes a sphere. Memoirs in the Académie royale de Belgique series, 1843–1868, collected as Statique expérimentale et théorique des liquides soumis aux seules forces moléculaires (Gauthier-Villars, 1873).
    Plateau's drop is narrower than it is usually made to carry, and it is not the geometry under dispute. There is no central attraction in it: the sphere is surface tension minimising area, a short-range force scaling with surface, where self-gravitation is a long-range volume interaction. The sequences diverge too — Plateau's spun drop goes sphere → oblate → torus, a self-gravitating rotating mass goes Maclaurin spheroid → Jacobi ellipsoid → pear. What Plateau shows is narrower and still worth showing: a liquid free of net body force takes the shape its dominant remaining force dictates.

    But none of that settles his question

    Here is the part most pages on this topic will not say, and it is the part that earns everything after it.

    Every demonstration above is equally consistent with a uniform downward field. A uniform g also gives a flat free surface. It also gives a paraboloid in a spun bucket. It also gives a sphere in Plateau's drop, because buoyancy cancels weight identically whether the field converges or not. It also permits the Moses effect and diamagnetic levitation. Not one of them distinguishes a converging field from a parallel one.

    Anyone who answers Miller with Newton's bucket has changed the subject.

    The four establish the principle — free surfaces follow the applied body force, and there is nothing for gravity to override. They establish nothing about whether Earth's field converges. The dispute survives them intact and reduces to one measurable question: do plumb lines 1,000 m apart differ in direction by 1,000/R radians — 32.376 arcseconds — or are they parallel?

    Miller states the globe model's answer correctly. Verbatim: "Heliocentrism says no, because the downward vector of spacetime curvature changes to a greater angle as we move from left to right on the diagram." His objection is evidential, not conceptual. He wants it demonstrated. That is fair.

    Measuring the vertical without touching water

    The local, star-free route: the vertical gravity gradient. The model-independent relation is Bruns' equation, ∂g/∂h = −2gJ + 4πGρ + 2ω², where J is the mean curvature of the level surface. In free air the density term vanishes and a measured gradient yields J directly, with no sphere assumed anywhere. That matters, because the version we would previously have written — "∇·g = 0 forces dg/dh = −2g/R" — is not true as stated. In Cartesian form ∇·g = 0 gives only ∂g_z/∂z = −(∂g_x/∂x + ∂g_y/∂y); you reach −2g/R only by additionally assuming spherical symmetry, which is assuming the conclusion. Bruns is the repair, and it strengthens the argument: what comes back is not evidence bearing on the disputed quantity, it is the disputed quantity, measured locally.

    The standard free-air value is 0.3086 mGal per metre. Inverting as R = 2g/(dg/dh) gives 6.356 × 10⁶ m, 0.24% from 6,371 km.

    Three qualifications, all cutting against us. 0.3086 mGal/m is a theoretical normal-field value computed from an assumed rotating ellipsoid, so recovering a near-polar-radius number from it is closer to definitional than triumphant — agreement between two constructs, not experimental accuracy. We also dropped the rotational term 2ω², itself a fraction of a percent of the gradient and so comparable to the agreement quoted. And real gradients are strongly site-dependent: the literature contains a measured vertical gradient of the opposite sign in a karstic sinkhole. The defensible claim is consistency within the few-percent site dependence. The instrument, too, is a relative gravimeter re-sited at a surveyed height difference, not an absolute FG5-X carried up a stepladder — the FG5-X's own measurement height needs a gradient correction to transfer. Its 2 µGal accuracy anchors how well gravity can be known; it does not describe the procedure.
    The astronomical route: deflection of the vertical. Comparing the plumb line against the stars at two stations gives the astronomical latitude difference directly. Digital zenith cameras (TZK2-D, Hannover; DIADEM, ETH Zurich) reach 0.08 arcsecond in a 20–25 minute field observation, 0.05″ in extended sessions. Against 32.376″, a two-station differential at σ = √2 × 0.08″ gives a signal-to-noise ratio near 290.

    We flag this as incomplete against Miller specifically. Reading a plumb line against the stars presumes stellar rays arrive parallel, and he disputes stellar distances at length elsewhere in the book. Presenting 32.4″ as decisive without naming that premise would read to him as question-begging, and fairly so. The gradient measurement carries no such premise, and it is the one we put forward.

    Why nobody builds the warehouse: water is a mediocre instrument at a kilometre

    Not because it would fail. Because at this scale water is exquisitely sensitive to things other than the one being measured. Sensitivity of the measurement, largest first, each as a fraction of the 19.62 mm signal. These are alternative magnitudes for each term under different conditions, not contributions to be added:

    TermSize% of signalCharacter

    Mean meniscus in a 2 mm "skinny" channel7.4 mm38%Common-mode; cancels differentially
    Optical reference bent by refraction, k = 0.203.92 mm20%Systematic, same sense as the signal
    Optical reference bent by refraction, k = 0.132.55 mm13%Systematic

    Earth-tide tilt at 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ rad0.24 mm1.2%Periodic, modellable
    Differential air pressure, 0.02 hPa0.20 mm1.0%Systematic
    Earth-tide tilt at 1 × 10⁻⁷ rad (Zhang's observed level)0.10 mm0.5%Periodic, modellable
    Thermal, 0.5 m depth, 1 K end-to-end0.104 mm0.53%Systematic
    Meniscus at mid-channel, 50 mm width0.40 µm0.002%Negligible
    Differential air pressure dominates, and it is the term everyone forgets. An open channel is a manometer. A pressure difference ΔP across its ends displaces the level by ΔP/ρg — 10.19 mm per hectopascal, more than half the entire signal. Weather-scale horizontal gradients run around 0.01 hPa/km, which would be tolerable. A kilometre-long enclosed hall is not weather. HVAC, a door opening at one end, stack effect driven by the very end-to-end temperature difference Miller is controlling, or wind loading on one gable produces tenths of a hectopascal without effort. At 0.1 hPa that is 1.02 mm, 5.2% of signal — and it is not random. It tracks time of day and wind direction.
    This is what the air in a half-filled pipe is for. The accelerator literature runs hydrostatic levelling systems half-filled, and the stated reasons are temperature insensitivity and settling time. The unstated one is that a continuous gas headspace running the length of the system equalises pressure along the run, so the manometer term goes away. An open channel with two ends open separately to a room has no such protection.
    Capillarity, and the one explicit design choice that hurts. He specifies "skinny," twice. Skinny is backwards. Between vertical walls a distance w apart, water climbs h = 2γcosθ/(ρgw). At γ = 0.0728 N/m, a 2 mm channel holds a 7.4 mm meniscus — 38% of the signal.

    The mean meniscus is not the problem; it is identical at every station and cancels differentially. The variation is. Since dh/dw = −h/w, a 2 mm channel changes height by 3.7 mm per millimetre of width change, so holding it to 1% of signal (0.196 mm) requires width uniform to 53 µm over 1,000 metres. Contact angle is worse: θ drifting 0° to 30° along the run costs 1.0 mm, and hysteresis on a real surface is not controllable at that level.

    The fix is cheap. Water's capillary length is λ = √(γ/ρg) = 2.73 mm, and beyond roughly ten of those the meniscus is a wall-attached feature decaying exponentially, not a bulk elevation: at 50 mm width the mid-channel elevation is 0.40 µm. A 50 mm channel solves capillarity outright. "Skinny" was the one specification that had to be reversed.

    Seiching. A closed channel rings at Merian's fundamental T = 2L/√(gh): 33.7 minutes at 100 mm depth over 1,000 m, 15.1 minutes at 500 mm. Zhang, Fukami & Matsui (JASRI/SPring-8, IWAA 2002) derive ω₁ = (π/l)√(gh) and tabulate a 1 km system at 44 mm depth at about 3,050 s against our 3,045 s — 0.19% — with their 500 m and 100 m entries reproduced to better than 0.3%.
    We deliberately do not transfer their settling time. Their "you need at least one hour before the disturbed water surface getting quiet for a one-kilometer system" is explicitly conditioned on 12 mm diameter pipes between sensors — viscous-damping-limited settling in a high-resistance geometry. The 50 mm × 100 mm open channel that defeating capillarity requires cuts that resistance by orders of magnitude. Damping falls with it and the ring-down is longer, not an hour — so their settling figure cannot be carried across to this channel without importing a geometry they never analysed. Damp deliberately, with the in-pipe breakwaters Schwarz published, or characterise the settling time yourself.
    Temperature — the confound he controlled for is the small one. A column of depth d whose temperature differs by ΔT from its neighbour sits higher by d·β·ΔT. At 20 °C, β ≈ 2.07 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, so 0.5 m depth and a full degree end to end gives 0.104 mm: 0.53% of signal. He was right that it is a confound and right to control it, and he could have controlled it far more loosely than the barometric term demands. And the instrument that looks like the obvious answer is not one. A compensated digital level defines its horizontal by gravity, so double-run levelling measures height differences along the equipotential. Run a Trimble DiNi down Miller's warehouse and it returns zero between middle and ends by construction — it is a second water level with a better readout. Its 0.3 mm/km specification is precision relative to the geoid, which is precision relative to the very surface in dispute. That figure is genuine, and useful for surveying the channel's supports. It is not an alternative to water here, and citing its 0.3 mm/km against a 19.6 mm signal mistakes a precision figure for an independent one.

    The reference problem: what a straight line is made of

    Measuring a sagitta requires a straight line, and the straight line is harder than the water.

    A taut wire is not a line. It is a catenary. Sag = ρ_m g L²/(8σ). Steel (ρ = 7,850 kg/m³) at 1 GPa over 1,000 m sags 9.63 m; at 2 GPa, essentially ultimate tensile strength, 4.81 m; carbon fibre (ρ = 1,600 kg/m³) at 3.5 GPa still sags 0.56 m (0.63 m if the fibre is taken at 1,800 kg/m³ — the figure is density-sensitive, which is itself part of the point). Against a 19.6 mm signal that is 30 to 500 times the thing being measured — and the catenary correction is computed from gravity, so even a corrected wire assumes what the experiment is meant to show. Wire offsets are a real technique over tens of metres with modelled sag. They are not a kilometre-scale straight line, and we have deleted them from our own recipe. Differential GPS is circular here. GPS solves positions in ECEF/WGS84, a geocentric ellipsoidal frame. Using it to realise the straight line against which you demonstrate Earth's curvature assumes the conclusion. LIGO may legitimately use DGPS for beam-tube alignment, because LIGO is not testing Earth's shape; it is building an interferometer on an assumed one. We would be testing it. The same instrument is admissible in one experiment and question-begging in the other, and the difference lies entirely in what is claimed. Any optical reference in air bends — and this is the optics half of his own chapter title. The refraction error on a sight-line is k·d²/8R: at the standard levelling coefficient k = 0.13 that is 2.55 mm over a kilometre, 13% of signal; at k = 0.20, 3.92 mm, 20%. And k is driven by the vertical air-temperature gradient, which above a temperature-controlled water surface inside a closed hall is precisely what has been engineered into existence. Refraction is not background nuisance here. It is a systematic that Miller's own temperature control helps create, carrying the same sign as the effect being sought.

    That leaves the evacuated optical line as the only non-question-begging kilometre-scale straight reference on the list. Not the wire, which sags. Not the GPS chord, which assumes the answer. A laser in a vacuum pipe — which is also, not coincidentally, how SLAC aligns a linac.

    One caution on those two coefficients. k = 0.13 is the standard coefficient for outdoor levelling and k = 0.20 a common upper bound for it; both are quoted here only to set a scale. A closed hall with a temperature-controlled water surface is not free atmosphere, and by the page's own argument its vertical gradient has been engineered rather than inherited — so the indoor coefficient could fall outside that range, and with a sufficiently strong inversion it could change sign, in which case the refraction error subtracts from the apparent signal instead of adding to it. We could not find a measured indoor coefficient for a facility of this kind, and the honest position is that this term needs measuring on site rather than assuming.

    What the straight machines already show

    Hydrostatic levelling systems are the professional version of Miller's channel, and they work extraordinarily well: Zhang, Fukami & Matsui report a movable HLS with resolution better than 1 micron, sensitive enough to see Earth tides at about 10⁻⁷ radians, and Schwarz (IWAA 1997, TESLA) a 5 µm capacitive sensor measuring reference-point heights over roughly 600 m to better than 0.1 mm.

    Why are HLS lines usually run in sections? We looked specifically for a source saying "Earth curvature" and did not find one. The sources say response time and temperature. We will not assert the curvature explanation, because we cannot source it.

    What curvature does govern is whether a water surface can reference a machine at all — and here Schwarz says the interesting thing outright. Of a 33 km collider tunnel: "In the vertical the axis of the tunnel follows the earth curvature except the first 6.5 km." That is a machine whose tunnel axis is designed to Earth's curvature over most of its length, with the exception called out explicitly, which is why HLS applies "in the part of the tunnel which is horizontal." A water surface realises an equipotential. It can reference a machine built to follow one; it cannot reference a machine built straight.

    LIGO is the fully documented case. LIGO-P000006: "The curvature of the Earth will cause the Earth's surface to deviate from the straight line propagated by light in vacuum by 1.25 meters over a 4 km path" — and that paper's trailing condition, if the line starts out level with the surface, is the tangent-versus-chord distinction stated by LIGO itself. Our d²/2R at 4,000 m gives 1.2557 m against their 1.25 m, 0.5% agreement, on the same eight-inches-per-mile geometry Miller invoked. (LIGO's public facts page rounds this to "nearly a meter"; the paper is the number to use.) The engineering consequence, in their words, is precision levelling of the concrete slab so the beam strikes the far mirror "and not a meter above it."

    Like for like against the warehouse, the comparison is sagitta to sagitta: over 4 km, d²/8R is 314 mm, 16 times the 19.62 mm he asks about. We say 16 and not 64, because comparing LIGO's tangent drop against a warehouse sagitta would commit the exact conflation this page spent a section explaining.

    Where this page could be wrong

    If a figure in his book draws a tangent rather than a chord. We read "the water in the middle is 3 inches higher compared to the left and right edges" as the sagitta. If the diagram draws a tangent at one end, his 3 inches is right for what he drew and only the wording is loose — a far smaller correction, and this page would say so. If someone has built it. We found no published account of a controlled-environment liquid-surface experiment at 500 m or longer, run to measure departure from a straight reference. If one exists we would lead with it rather than concede his literal claim. If site dependence swamps the prediction. 6,371 km is a global mean; what sets a specific channel's curvature is the local radius in its own azimuth at its own latitude, varying at the percent level with both. Worse, the channel follows the geoid, not a sphere, and local mass anomalies tilt the plumb line: against 32.376″ of convergence over the kilometre, a deflection gradient of an arcsecond per kilometre is already a 3% perturbation, and five arcseconds per kilometre would be 15% — we could not source a figure for the gradient at any specific site, so this is a caution rather than a correction. The honest prediction for a real site is 19.6 mm plus a site-dependent few percent, never 19.6202 mm to six figures. If Bruns' equation smuggles in something we have not noticed. It is the load-bearing beam here. If a measured free-air vertical gradient can be reproduced by a non-convergent field configuration, the strongest star-free claim weakens and the astronomical route — with its stellar-distance premise, which Miller rejects — is all that remains. If we have the barometric term wrong for a real hall. Our 0.1 hPa is an engineering estimate, not a measurement inside one. If a well-sealed kilometre hall holds its ends to 0.02 hPa, the manometer error falls to 0.20 mm and the objection softens from a reason into a design requirement.

    The concession we can afford

    Built properly, Miller's experiment would work.

    Make the channel 50 mm wide rather than skinny, so capillarity retreats to the walls and mid-channel elevation falls to 0.40 µm. Give it a continuous sealed gas headspace along the full run, so the ends cannot sit at different pressures. Damp it deliberately with in-pipe breakwaters. Hold the temperature to about a degree, looser than he supposed. Then reference it — not to a spirit level or a digital level, both of which realise the same equipotential the water does and would read zero by construction, but to an evacuated optical line: the only kilometre-scale straight reference that neither sags under gravity nor solves for position in a frame that assumes the answer.

    It would read 19.6 mm. Not 3 inches. And 19.6 mm is a real, resolvable, unambiguous signal.

    So the honest closing claim is not that his experiment is a non-answer. A properly built channel referenced to an evacuated straight line genuinely does measure whether the local vertical converges. The claim is narrower: water is a worse instrument than the alternatives. It is a slow, thermally fussy, barometrically exposed way to obtain a number a gravimeter gives you locally in an afternoon, and that LIGO pays for in concrete every day at sixteen times the magnitude.

    The absence Miller correctly identifies is real. It is an absence of reason to, not an absence of ability to.

    And on the framing: he is not wrong that water follows the field, and his book says so in his own words — "the downward vector of spacetime curvature changes to a greater angle as we move from left to right on the diagram." That is the globe model's claim, stated accurately, by him. Nothing has to be strong enough to bend hydrostatics, because hydrostatics is defined relative to whatever body force is applied. The disagreement is entirely about whether the local vertical converges — a question you settle with gravimeters, and could settle, far more expensively, with a very carefully built kilometre of water.

    Open questions

    • — Does the diagram on page 89 of Miller's book draw a tangent at one end or a chord between both? If a tangent, his 3 inches is correct for what he drew and only the verbal description is loose — a much smaller correction than the one this page makes.
    • — Has anyone published a controlled-environment liquid-surface experiment at 500 m or longer, run specifically to measure departure from an independently realised straight reference? We found none. A counterexample would falsify Miller's literal claim and would have to lead the page.
    • — What differential pressure does a real kilometre-long enclosed hall actually sustain end to end under HVAC, door cycling, stack effect and one-sided wind loading? Our 0.1 hPa is an engineering estimate. The whole error budget hinges on it, and it is measurable with two barometers before any water is poured.
    • — What is the actual ring-down time of a 50 mm × 100 mm open channel at 1 km? Zhang's one hour applies to 12 mm pipes and does not transfer. Nobody appears to have computed or measured the low-resistance case.
    • — Can a measured free-air vertical gravity gradient be reproduced by any non-convergent field configuration? Bruns' equation is the load-bearing beam of the star-free argument, and we would want this closed rather than assumed.
    • — Is there any published measured Moses-effect depression in a large-bore magnet? Our computable values are idealised ceilings roughly ten times the reported small-magnet values, and we quote none as a result.
