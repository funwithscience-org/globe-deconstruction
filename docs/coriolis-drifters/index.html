One Spin, Written Across Every Latitude

Fifteen weather buoys, no intention of proving anything, and the length of the day to one part in a thousand.

Free-floating ocean drifters do not travel in straight lines. Riding on top of whatever current is carrying them is a small circular wobble — an inertial oscillation — and its two properties are pure kinematics of a rotating body. The loops turn clockwise in the northern hemisphere and counter-clockwise in the southern, reversing at the equator. And the time to complete one loop follows T = 11.97 h ÷ sin φ — stretching toward infinity at the equator, tightening toward both poles. This page tests both against public data from NOAA's Global Drifter Program: buoys deployed for weather and climate work, by people not arguing with anyone about the shape of the Earth.

Prediction confirmedWhat fifteen buoys establish

Loop direction was correct in 15 out of 15 cases — every northern buoy clockwise, every southern buoy counter-clockwise. Loop period matched the sine-of-latitude law within 10% for 13 of 15. Fitting the one free constant across all fifteen recovers 11.98 hours against a true value of 11.97, which is half a sidereal day — so these buoys measure the length of Earth's day to 0.1%, having been launched to do nothing of the kind.

1 · Three models make three different pictures

What makes this decisive rather than merely consistent is that the competing models don't disagree by a little. They predict qualitatively different figures, and the difference is visible without any statistics at all.

Take the local Coriolis parameter, f, which sets both the rate and the handedness of the loops. It is twice the component of the planet's rotation along your local vertical:

  • — A stationary Earth. Nothing rotates, so f = 0 everywhere. There are no inertial loops at any latitude. Drifters follow the current and nothing else.
  • — A flat turntable spinning about an axis through the pole. The rotation axis is perpendicular to the surface everywhere on the disc, so f = 2Ω at every location: loops of identical period, roughly 12 hours, turning the same way from the centre to the rim. Nothing distinguishes one circle of latitude from another, and nothing at all happens at the equator.
  • — A spinning sphere. Only the vertical component of the rotation counts, so f = 2Ω sin φ. Loops are fastest at the poles, slow to nothing at the equator, and reverse handedness across it, because the local vertical points the opposite way relative to the spin.
The handedness flip is the part that cannot be explained away. A magnitude can always be argued about — instruments drift, currents interfere, someone picked the buoys. A sign reversal at a line the buoys have no way of sensing is a different kind of evidence. Nothing in a drifter knows where the equator is. It has no compass, no map and no opinion. Yet every one of the seven northern buoys loops one way and every one of the eight southern buoys loops the other.

3 · The buoys measure the length of the day

The relationship has exactly one constant in it. Write the law as T = A ÷ sin φ and ask what value of A the fifteen buoys actually imply, rather than assuming it:

MethodRecovered AError against truth
Unweighted mean12.019 h0.4%
Median12.017 h0.4%
Weighted by measurement confidence11.980 h0.1%
True value (half a sidereal day)11.967 h—

Doubling the weighted figure gives a sidereal day of 23.96 hours against an actual 23.93. For comparison, Arthur Compton's 1913 laboratory apparatus — a ring of water flipped on a bench, purpose-built to measure exactly this — got the day to about 1%. Fifteen buoys bobbing around the ocean for other reasons do better by an order of magnitude.

Two details are worth more than the headline. The confidence weighting was computed from in-band spectral energy before any of this, with no knowledge of the answer, and applying it improves the fit from 0.4% to 0.1% — which is how an honest quality metric behaves and is not how a fudge factor behaves. And simply discarding the two weakest buoys makes the result worse (0.6%), so the good number is not coming from quietly dropping inconvenient data.

4 · Method, in enough detail to repeat

Everything here comes from a public archive and can be reproduced from scratch.

  • 1 Source. NOAA AOML Global Drifter Program, hourly product gdp_hourly_velocities, served over ERDDAP at data.pmel.noaa.gov. Public domain. Fields used: ID, time, latitude, longitude, ve, vn.
  • 2 Per buoy, pull hourly eastward and northward velocity across a multi-month window — about 2,880 hourly samples for a full run.
  • 3 Form the complex velocity w = (ve − mean) + i(vn − mean). Removing the mean strips out the background current so the loops stand clear of the drift.
  • 4 Rotary spectrum by FFT. Negative frequency is clockwise, positive is counter-clockwise. The sign of the dominant peak is the handedness — it is read off the data, not assigned.
  • 5 Locate the peak within an adaptive band of 0.6 to 1.7 times the predicted frequency. In-band energy ratio between the two senses of rotation gives the confidence figure.
One unit trap, recorded because it cost a run. The inertial frequency 2Ω sin φ is in radians per second. Converting to cycles per hour requires dividing by 2π and multiplying by 3600. Omitting the 2π puts the predicted frequency 6.28× too high, drags the search band away from the signal, and returns confident nonsense for the handedness. Correct form: f = Ω sin|φ| / π × 3600 cycles/hour. Sanity check it against T ≈ 69.6 h at 9.9° and 13.4 h at 63°.

5 · What could be wrong with this

Stated before anyone else has to.

Fifteen buoys is a ladder, not a distribution. The airtight version of this claim is statistical — hemisphere energy asymmetry across hundreds of drifters, binned by latitude, crossing zero at the equator. One buoy per rung demonstrates the effect and traces the curve; it does not yet pin down the scatter. That larger run is the obvious next piece of work and is not done here.

Several periods are a few percent off, and one is about 20% off. That is the buoy at 22.7°S, and the discrepancy is real physics rather than noise: near-inertial waves sit slightly above f, shifted by the vorticity of the background flow they are embedded in. The effective frequency is roughly f + ζ/2, so a buoy caught in an anticyclonic eddy loops faster than its latitude alone predicts. These periods are deliberately left uncorrected. Correcting them would tighten the fit and would also be the single easiest place to hide a thumb on the scale.

Velocities are a derived product. The ve/vn fields are computed by interpolating satellite position fixes and differentiating them, with quality control applied. The inertial signal is present in the raw fixes too, but anyone who wants to argue “interpolation artifact” is entitled to, and the clean answer is to redo one buoy from raw fixes. Also not done here.

The band-pass is for display, not for manufacturing the result. The unfiltered rotary spectrum already shows the hemisphere asymmetry at roughly ten to one. Filtering makes the loops legible on a chart; it does not create them.

6 · Why the equator is the null and not the maximum

The natural objection: if Coriolis comes from the Earth's spin, and the spin is fastest at the equator — a thousand miles an hour — shouldn't the effect be strongest there rather than absent?

The acceleration is a = −2Ω × v, and everything follows from which way that cross product points. For horizontal deflection only the vertical component of Ω contributes, giving f = 2Ω sin φ — the same for any heading at a given latitude, and zero at the equator. At the equator the rotation axis lies flat in the local horizontal plane: motion north or south is parallel to it, so the cross product vanishes, and motion east or west produces a force that is vertical rather than sideways.

That vertical part does not disappear — it goes as cos φ and is maximal at the equator. It is the Eötvös effect: travel east and you weigh very slightly less, travel west and slightly more. One cross product, two projections, opposite latitude dependence. The horizontal one dies at the equator; the vertical one peaks there.
