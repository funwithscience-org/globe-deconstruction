Zero Speed at the Axis

Question 11, the Compton generator, and the difference between spinning slowly and not spinning.

Question 11 rests on a fact that is entirely correct: at the rotational pole, the ground is not travelling anywhere. Its speed through space, in the sense of miles per hour across the surface, is zero. From that the question draws a conclusion — that there is therefore nothing there for an experiment to detect — and it is that step, not the fact, that fails. Zero tangential speed is not zero rotation. The pole turns in place, at the same 15.04° per hour as everywhere else, and because local rotation experiments respond to the vertical component of that turning, the pole is where they read strongest. The question names the one place on Earth where the effect is maximal and describes it as the place where nothing happens.

Globe Deconstruction? — Question 11, p. 54 “If you travel to the exact location of the rotational south pole, why does a high-altitude time-lapse show no movement of the surrounding Earth landscape relative to the camera? Keep in mind that the rotational speed of the air at the axis point would be zero. Why has no scientist tested the Compton generator in Antarctica?”

Premise invertedExperiment already runThe short version

The camera and the landscape share the same rotation, so of course they show no relative motion — that is true at every latitude, not just the pole, and the thing you would film moving is the sky. Angular rate does not fall to zero at the axis; only linear speed does. A Compton generator at the South Pole would read 1.54× stronger than where Compton himself ran it. A Foucault pendulum — the same physics in a different medium — was operated at the South Pole, in 2001. And the ocean performs the equivalent measurement continuously at every latitude on Earth, with results that recover the length of the day to 0.1%.

1 · The longer version of the same argument

Question 11 is a summary. The chapter behind it runs from p. 178 to p. 194 and makes the case at length: a table on p. 190 giving air speeds of 1,040 mph at six feet up rising to 1,050 mph at 37 miles, with the objection that “the higher layers of air would have to travel mechanically faster to keep up”; then p. 191, “Does this synchronized rotation happen at all points of the ball? — 0 mph air speed at axis”; then p. 192, which puts it most directly:

“At the exact axial point, what makes a drone rotate in sync with the ground if it is not attached to it? Axial air speed: 0 mph.” — p. 192

The same chapter then examines the Foucault pendulum (pp. 192–194) and ring-laser gyroscopes (pp. 183–187). What it never does, anywhere across five hundred pages, is say what a Compton generator is. The term appears exactly once — in Question 11 — and is never defined, described or explained. A reader has no way to evaluate the question as posed, so the first useful thing this page can do is simply answer it.

2 · What a Compton generator actually is

It is a hollow ring of tubing, filled with water and sealed. Arthur Compton built the first in 1913 from one-inch brass tube bent into a circle about nine inches in radius, with fine droplets of coal oil suspended in the water and a microscope trained on a small viewing window.

You lay the ring flat and wait for the water to come completely to rest. Then you flip the whole thing through 180° about a horizontal diameter — over, and flat again — and look through the microscope. The water is now drifting steadily around the tube.

Lying flat, the ring's axis points up, and the water is quietly turning with the Earth about that axis at a rate of Ω sin φ. Flip it and the axis points down, so the tube is now turning at −Ω sin φ, while the water's angular momentum has not changed. Relative to the tube, the water circulates at twice the original rate. It is a Foucault pendulum with the swinging bob replaced by a fluid:

ū = 2ΩR sin φ

Note the sin φ. The effect is zero at the equator and maximum at the poles. Running Compton's own nine-inch ring:

LocationDrift speedNotes
Equator0nothing to detect
Princeton, where Compton worked21.6 µm/s (1.3 mm/min)gave latitude to 3%, day length to ~1%
South Pole33.3 µm/s (2.0 mm/min)1.54× stronger than Princeton
So the question has the sign backwards. Antarctica is not where a Compton generator would fail to register. It is where it would register most strongly — along with a Foucault pendulum, which at the pole precesses in exactly one sidereal day, the fastest rate available anywhere on Earth.

There is a second orientation worth knowing about, because it makes the pole better still rather than merely stronger. Stand the ring vertical with its axis pointing north–south and rotate about the vertical, and the reading follows 2ΩR cos φ instead — which at the pole is zero. One orientation gives you the maximum signal and the other gives you nothing, from the same apparatus in the same session. That is a built-in control against the obvious objection that flipping the ring simply sloshes the water: handling artifacts do not know which way the ring was facing.

3 · Angular rate versus linear speed

The premise — “the rotational speed of the air at the axis point would be zero” — is true of one quantity and false of the other, and the whole question turns on which is which.

At the equatorAt the pole
Tangential speed (mph across the surface)~1,040 mph0
Angular rate (degrees per hour)15.04°/h15.04°/h
Vertical component, Ω sin φ — what local experiments feel0maximum

A rigid body turning about an axis has the same angular velocity everywhere in it; only the distance from the axis, and therefore the linear speed, varies. The pole does not stop rotating. It rotates in place, and a spinning top does not become stationary at its own tip.

Which of the two matters depends on the experiment. Something being carried past you cares about linear speed. A Foucault pendulum, a Compton generator, a ring-laser gyroscope and a drifting ocean buoy all care about the angular rate resolved onto the local vertical — and that quantity is Ω sin φ, largest exactly where the linear speed is smallest.

4 · The time-lapse, and what would have to be true for it to show anything

Question 11 opens by asking why a high-altitude time-lapse at the rotational pole shows no movement of the surrounding landscape relative to the camera. p. 192 puts the same point in a form that makes the underlying reasoning visible: what makes a drone rotate in sync with the ground if it is not attached to it?

Set out in full, the argument runs like this. At the axis the air has no speed — nothing is being carried anywhere. A camera sitting up in that air is therefore not being dragged around by anything. So if the ground below really were rotating, the camera ought to hold its heading while the landscape turned underneath it, and a long enough exposure should capture that turning. No such turning is observed. Therefore the ground is not turning.

Every step of that follows — provided the camera is genuinely decoupled from the Earth.

That is the load-bearing assumption, and it is the only one the argument cannot do without. It fails in a different way depending on where the camera actually is.

If the camera is on the ice — which is where cameras usually are — then it and the landscape are bolted to the same rotating object and share its motion exactly. No relative movement, ever. That is equally true in Reykjavík, Nairobi or a back garden in Indiana; nothing about it is polar, and it tells you nothing about whether anything is rotating.

If the camera is airborne, the decoupling still doesn't happen, because the air is not stationary either. Zero tangential speed at the axis is not zero angular rate: the polar air column turns in place at the same 15.04° per hour as everywhere else, and anything floating in it turns with it. A drone additionally holds its own heading by design. There is nothing for it to fall out of sync with.

And if the camera really were decoupled — mounted on something that held a fixed orientation in space rather than relative to the ground — then the prediction is correct, and the landscape genuinely would rotate beneath it. It would do so at 15.04° per hour, which is 0.25° per minute. Across a typical drone flight of twenty to forty minutes that is five to ten degrees. Nobody is going to notice five degrees by eye in a shaky aerial shot, which is why this observation has never troubled anybody.

The fix is to film for longer, or to point the camera up. Six hours of that rotation is 90° and unmistakable. And a time-lapse aimed at the sky from the South Pole gives one of the cleanest results in observational astronomy — every star tracing a perfect concentric circle about the zenith, one full turn per sidereal day. That film has been shot many times. It is the same rotation, recorded against a reference that isn't bolted to the thing doing the rotating.

The deeper point is that this argument does not fail because it is silly. It fails because the effect is real but slow, and the eye is the wrong instrument. Put something sensitive enough on that decoupled mount and it registers the rotation immediately — which is precisely what a ring-laser gyroscope is, and precisely why the chapter has to spend pp. 183–187 arguing that those instruments are measuring something else instead.

5 · The ocean has already run this experiment, at every latitude

The strongest answer to “why has nobody tested this” is that a far better version of the test runs continuously, for free, and the data is public. Ocean drifters trace small circular loops on top of whatever current carries them — inertial oscillations, driven by exactly the physics a Compton generator is built to reveal. Their period follows the same sin φ law.

Fifteen NOAA drifting buoys, deployed for weather and climate work and not to argue with anybody, give this:

  • — 15 of 15 correct on handedness — every northern buoy looping clockwise, every southern buoy counter-clockwise, reversing across a line no buoy can detect.
  • — 13 of 15 within 10% of the predicted period.
  • — Fitting the single free constant recovers a sidereal day of 23.96 hours against an actual 23.93 — 0.1%, roughly ten times better than Compton's laboratory apparatus managed.

A stationary Earth predicts no loops at all. A flat turntable predicts loops of identical period and identical direction at every latitude, with nothing happening at the equator. A spinning sphere predicts the observed pattern. The three models are not close, and the full comparison — along with the data, method, and the places where the fit is imperfect — is set out here:

→ One Spin, Written Across Every Latitude — fifteen buoys, three models, and the length of the day recovered to 0.1% from public NOAA data.

This also disposes of the Antarctic framing entirely. You do not need a polar measurement to know what a polar instrument would read, because the curve is pinned from both hemispheres and extrapolates to 11.97 hours at the pole. Fifteen buoys that never went near Antarctica already answer the question.

6 · And it has been tested at the pole

Not with a ring of water, but with the pendulum version of the same physics. In the winter of 2001, Mike Town (University of Washington), John Bird (York University) and R. Allan Baker (Sonoma State) hung a Foucault pendulum down the six-storey stairwell of the new Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station while it was still under construction — a 33 m wire carrying a 25 kg bob, in a sealed shaft where no moving air could disturb it. They measured a precession period of 24 hours ± 50 minutes against a predicted 23 h 56 m, turning clockwise viewed from above the South Pole, and got g = 9.85 ± 0.03 m/s² along the way. Their own account is here, originally posted to Baker's university page in October 2001.

On the strength of that source, since this page asks for sourcing from others. We could find no peer-reviewed publication of this experiment. What exists is the experimenters' own write-up, plus secondary summaries that add the physical details above. The ±50-minute error bar is wide, and it was a station experiment run in a stairwell by people with day jobs, not a metrology campaign. So it is offered for exactly one purpose — answering “nobody has tried this at the pole” — and not as a precision result. If you want the best measurements of Earth's rotation, they come from ring lasers, which resolve it to parts in 10⁸, and from the drifter array above, which needs no expedition at all. If anyone can point us at a journal version of the 2001 pendulum, we will add it.

As for the Compton generator specifically: it is a 1913 teaching demonstration accurate to about 1%, and ring-laser gyroscopes now measure the same rotation continuously to parts in 10⁸. Nobody hauling a brass water torus to Antarctica is not a gap in the evidence. It is a 112-year-old instrument having been superseded — and notably, the modern separating test is Compton's test: rotate the instrument through 180° and the Earth signal reverses sign while any instrument bias stays put. That method is in continuous industrial use. It is simply no longer made of brass and water.

7 · The altitude claim, and what would produce it

The chapter's strongest empirical move is on pp. 183–187: a fibre-optic gyroscope reportedly read differently at different altitudes on the same line of latitude — a variance of “more than a full degree per hour” between Pike's Peak at 14,115 ft and the Eastern Plains at 4,000 ft — which is taken as showing the instrument is not measuring Earth's rotation at all. The globe model does predict no altitude dependence here, so the prediction is stated correctly. The question is whether the measurement is.

Credit where it is due, because it is unusual: the book says plainly that the data is unsourced. p. 187 reads, “I could not find the formal document with their exact measurements. We need multiple teams to perform this to see if the results are true.” That is more candour than this genre usually manages, and it also means the central table is, by the author's own assessment, the weakest evidence in the chapter.

Two things would produce the reported variance without any new physics.

Mounting tilt. A gyroscope with its sensitive axis vertical reads Ω sin(φ + tilt). At the stated latitude of 38.84°N the true signal is 9.433°/h, and the sensitivity to tilt is 0.204°/h per degree. So:

Mounting tiltReadingApparent variance
0° (perfect)9.433°/h—
2°9.836°/h+0.40°/h
5°10.418°/h+0.99°/h

Under five degrees of tilt reproduces the entire claimed effect. Levelling an instrument to better than a degree on a mountain summit, in cold and wind, is not something that happens by default — and a 10,000-foot altitude change also brings a large temperature swing, which is the other thing fibre-optic gyro bias tracks.

The control is not clean either. The comparison is presented as same-latitude, but the table lists Guanella Pass at 39.59°N against 38.84°N for the other two sites. That difference alone predicts 0.155°/h of variance from latitude, before anything else is considered.

Whether gyroscopes detect Earth's rotation at all — ring lasers, the Sagnac effect, instrument grades and drift figures, and the commercial deployments that depend on it — is covered separately: → Gyroscopes and Earth's rotation.

8 · The Foucault objections are real, and the citation runs backwards

p. 194 lists reasons Foucault pendulums are hard to run: an elliptical swing precesses on its own through Airy precession, a suspension slightly stiffer in one direction biases the path, temperature changes the wire, draughts push the bob, traffic vibration distorts it, and a misaligned electromagnetic drive can steer a museum pendulum into a false rate. Every one of those is true. Foucault pendulums are genuinely finicky, this is well known to the people who build them, and anyone who has seen a badly-set-up one has seen it produce nonsense.

What none of it explains is why the artifacts should trace a curve. Those error sources have no relationship to latitude. The precession rate does — it follows sin φ, from 37 hours at Princeton to 26.6 at Reykjavík to 23.9 at the pole. A stiff suspension does not know what latitude it is at.

One note on the source. The book cites Heike Kamerlingh Onnes's 1879 doctoral thesis as documenting why these pendulums fail. That thesis is titled “Nieuwe Bewijzen voor de aswenteling der aarde” — “New Proofs for the Axial Rotation of the Earth.” Onnes catalogued the error sources precisely so the experiment could be done properly and Earth's rotation established more rigorously. The error analysis is real and is worth conceding; the work it comes from reaches the opposite conclusion to the one it is cited for. (He also won his Nobel in 1913, thirty-four years after the thesis, for liquefying helium.)

9 · What would actually settle it

  • — Re-run the gyroscope comparison with a levelling protocol. Record instrument tilt to a tenth of a degree at each site, and log temperature. If the altitude variance survives that, it becomes interesting. This is cheap and the author has already asked for it himself.
  • — Flip the gyroscope 180° at each site. The Earth signal reverses sign; bias does not. This separates the two without needing a second location at all — and it is Compton's 1913 method.
  • — Publish the raw measurements. The table on p. 187 cannot be checked by anyone, including its author.
  • — Pull the drifter data. It is public, free, and needs no equipment. If the loops do not reverse at the equator, the sphere model is in serious trouble — and the check takes an afternoon.

The honest bottom line

The fact at the centre of Question 11 is correct: at the axis, surface speed is zero. The inference is not. Angular rate is undiminished there, local rotation experiments respond to angular rate, and the pole is consequently where a Compton generator reads hardest — 1.54× the signal Compton himself worked with, plus a free null control in the perpendicular orientation. A Foucault pendulum has in fact been run at the South Pole. And the ocean runs the equivalent experiment continuously at every latitude, returning the length of the day to a tenth of a percent and reversing its handedness at the equator, which neither a stationary Earth nor a spinning disc can produce. Nothing here needs a trip to Antarctica.
