    Sunlight bends in air, and the bend is about one arcminute

    Q12, page 55 — what atmospheric refraction actually does to a shadow-angle argument, measured in arcseconds.

    Unreviewed first draft. This page is published for review only. It has not been through the editorial pass the rest of this site has had, it is not linked as an answer in the catalogue, and it is marked noindex. Known outstanding issues on this page:
    • The 17.04° figure currently takes its ground distance from an integration of the WGS84 meridian — the conclusion in parametric form — while the text calls it surveyed ground. Needs rebuilding from measured degree lengths.
    Numbers here have been cross-checked against their research record, but treat any figure as provisional until this notice is removed.
    Globe Deconstruction? — Question 12, p. 55 · his words, quoted “What scientist proved that sunlight moves in a perfectly straight line through hundreds or thousands of miles of gas? Is this an incorrect assumption when measuring shadow angles to dismiss the possibility of a flat Earth?”

    Q12 asks who established that sunlight travels in a perfectly straight line through hundreds of miles of atmosphere, and whether that assumption invalidates shadow-angle arguments. Nobody established it, because it is false: air bends light, the bend has been measured since the 1580s, and it is inside every serious shadow-angle calculation already. This page grants the premise in full, then measures the bend. At the sun elevations shadow-angle work actually uses, the differential refraction between the two Eratosthenes stations comes to about 7.5 arcseconds against a signal of 25,596 arcseconds — a 0.029 per cent perturbation, roughly seventy times smaller than the error bar the ancient measurement already carried from not knowing the length of a stadion. We also concede, without hedging, that two stations cannot decide between a far sun on a globe and a near sun over a plane; that degeneracy is real and the usual textbook telling overstates the case. What breaks it is over-determination across latitude, using a discriminator from which the flat model's one free parameter cancels exactly. Run that comparison against surveys made in the eighteenth century to settle an argument between Newton and Cassini, and the flat-plus-near-sun model needs the atmosphere to bend sunlight by 17.04 degrees at the Arctic Circle. The atmosphere supplies 0.037 degrees there, and 0.575 degrees at its absolute maximum. Q12 as printed asks whether straight-line propagation is “an incorrect assumption when measuring shadow angles to dismiss the possibility of a flat Earth” — so shadow angles are the argument, and that is what this page answers.

    Where this lands

    The premise of Q12 is correct and we concede it entirely: sunlight does not travel in a straight line through the atmosphere, nobody ever established that it does, and anyone who has answered this question by asserting straight-line propagation has answered it wrongly. The question can only be settled by measuring the bend. When we measure it, the bend at the sun elevations shadow-angle arguments use is worth a few arcseconds. Across the Alexandria–Syene baseline the differential refraction is about 7.5 arcseconds against a 25,596-arcsecond signal, and the non-parallelism of the solar rays across that baseline is about 1.16 arcseconds. Neither correction reaches the conclusion. Miller's stronger point — that one pair of shadow angles is degenerate between models — is right, and we grant it in section one rather than defending it. The degeneracy is broken not by refining the pair but by adding stations, using a ratio from which the flat model's sun height cancels identically. Against the measured length of a degree of latitude, the flat-plus-near-sun model is out by a factor of six at the Arctic Circle, and closing that gap by optics would require 17.04 degrees of atmospheric bending where the atmosphere delivers 0.037. The same measured refractive index of air produces both numbers; it cannot be trusted for one and refused for the other.

    What the question gets right

    Q12 is quoted in full at the head of this page. It names shadow angles explicitly, which fixes what has to be answered here and rules out some of the other arguments this question is often confused with. If the argument on that page differs materially from the question as we catalogued it, parts of what follows will miss their target, and we would like to be told.

    With that said, the premise is correct, and there are five separate things in it we concede.

    One. Sunlight does not travel in a straight line through air, and nobody established that it does. Atmospheric refraction is real, it acts continuously along the whole path, and it has been measured for more than four centuries. The first accurate measurements were made by Tycho Brahe in the sixteenth century. If the answer to Q12 were "light goes straight," the answer would be wrong. Two. The question is the right kind of question. A shadow-angle argument does rest on an optical assumption about the intervening air, and that assumption ought to be stated and quantified. Every textbook presentation of Eratosthenes we could find states the parallel-ray assumption in passing and then never puts a number on it, and never mentions refraction at all. That is a real gap in how the argument is taught, and the burden of closing it is ours, not his. Three. A single pair of shadow angles genuinely cannot decide between the two models. The reported figures — 5,000 stadia, about 788 km at 157.5 m to the stadion, and 7.2° of angle — fit a spherical circumference of 39,375 km exactly as well as they fit a flat plane with the sun 6,234 km overhead. Two observations, two free parameters, one equation. Anyone who says two sticks proved the Earth round has overstated it. Four. Near the ground and near the horizon, refraction is not merely large but genuinely erratic. Hirt and colleagues ran motorised total stations on an 800 m baseline, 1.8 m above grass, for 33 hours, and measured the refraction coefficient k swinging from −4 to +16 on sunny days against a textbook value of about 0.13, with wave-like fluctuations of amplitude 1 to 1.5 on ten- to thirty-minute timescales. For grazing sight lines the standard number can be wrong by two orders of magnitude. Five. Engineers who need light to travel genuinely straight over kilometres remove the air. SLAC's two-mile linear accelerator was aligned through a 24-inch evacuated pipe held at roughly 10–25 torr, specifically "to avoid distortion and translation of the image due to refraction of the light in the atmosphere," reaching 0.025 mm accuracy. Asked whether a 250 m system would need vacuum, Herrmannsfeldt answered: "you will need some kind of vacuum, yes... 250 m is too long for reliable work."
    That fifth item is a concession, not a rebuttal, and we place it here deliberately. A physicist who will not trust air over a 250 m horizontal sight line is telling you the effect is not negligible in general. It is negligible for a specific geometry, and this page has to earn that geometry rather than assume it.
    The question therefore cannot be answered by defending straight-line light. It has to be answered by measuring the bend and showing what size of conclusion it can and cannot overturn.

    The bend is measured, and the path in between does not matter

    The phrase "hundreds of miles of atmosphere" does real rhetorical work, because it invites you to imagine an unknown with hundreds of miles of accumulated error in it. The physics deletes that unknown, and it does so exactly.

    Model the atmosphere as horizontal layers of differing density — plane-stratified, optical surfaces parallel to the ground. Apply Snell's law at each interface. The refracted angle leaving one layer is the incident angle entering the next, so the product n·sin z comes out identical at every point along the ray, where z is the ray's angle from the vertical. Every intermediate layer cancels. Only the two endpoints survive.

    The total bending does not depend on the density profile, the temperature profile, the scale height, the number of layers, or how many miles of air the ray crossed.

    It depends on two things: the refractive index of air where the observer is standing, and the zenith angle. That is the whole of it. Under this model the refraction reduces to a one-line formula, r = (n − 1)·tan z, which Andrew T. Young's treatment states is accurate to better than one part in ten thousand while r itself stays under about half a degree — a domain that covers everything above roughly 10° of elevation, which is to say everything a noon shadow measurement ever uses.

    The sign is also known, within a stated domain. In an atmosphere stratified horizontally with density falling monotonically upward — the ordinary case for a high sun on a clear day — the ray curves toward the denser air beneath it, so the source appears higher than its geometric direction and the shadow is shortened. That domain matters and we will not overstate it. Strong surface inversions reverse the local gradient, and horizontal gradients break the stratification assumption entirely; those are the conditions that produce mirages, and section nine is about them. But for the geometry under discussion, refraction is a correction with a known direction, not a scrambler.

    Two context figures for "hundreds of miles," neither of them load-bearing. The entire atmosphere compressed to sea-level density is 8.43 km thick: P/(ρg) = 101,325 / (1.225 × 9.80665) = 8,434 m, a column mass of 10,332 kg/m². And the photovoltaic industry's reference spectrum, ASTM G173, is defined at an absolute air mass of 1.5 — one and a half times the vertical column — at a solar zenith angle of 48.19°, since 1/cos(48.19°) = 1.5000. That is the number the entire solar panel industry uses for how much atmosphere sunlight crosses. We offer these only because the phrase invites a picture; the invariant above makes the path length irrelevant regardless.

    The number, from a bench, with no astronomy in it

    The refractive index of air is not an astronomical quantity. It is a metrology quantity, and it is measured by people who do not care about the sky.

    The Ciddor dispersion relation for standard air is

    > n − 1 = 0.05792105/(238.0185 − λ⁻²) + 0.00167917/(57.362 − λ⁻²), λ in µm.

    At λ = 0.589 µm, λ⁻² = 2.8820; the first term is 2.46331 × 10⁻⁴, the second 3.0822 × 10⁻⁵, and the sum is 2.7715 × 10⁻⁴ — standard air at 15 °C, 101.325 kPa, 450 ppm CO₂. Scaled to 0 °C by the density ratio 288.15/273.15 = 1.0549, it becomes 2.9237 × 10⁻⁴, which is the figure our celestial-globes page already uses.

    NIST serves this relation through its Engineering Metrology Toolbox, because laser interferometric length measurement of gauge blocks cannot be done without correcting for the optical path in air. It was measured so that machine shops and national metrology institutes can agree on what a millimetre is.

    Now feed it into r = (n − 1)·tan z, and compare against Bennett's 1982 navigation formula, which is fitted to real sky observations and does not use the small-angle approximation.

    Conventions, stated once and held for the rest of the page. We quote Bennett with its pressure–temperature scale factor set to unity — that is, the bare 1/tan(h + 7.31/(h + 4.4)) — which corresponds to about 1010 hPa and 10 °C. At 1013.25 hPa and 12 °C the factor 0.28*P*/(T + 273) becomes 0.9955 and the 45° value would be 59.4″ rather than 59.7″. Every Bennett number below is the unscaled one. For the plane-parallel column we use 15 °C standard air, n − 1 = 2.7715 × 10⁻⁴, and give the 0 °C column beside it so the reader can see how much of the spread between the two methods is simply the temperature each convention assumes.

    The full Bennett column, for scale: 34.5′ at the horizon, 9.88′ at 5°, 5.39′ at 10°, 2.70′ at 20°, 1.72′ at 30°, 0.99′ at 45°, 0.57′ at 60°, 0.27′ at 75°, 0.13′ at 82.5°, 0.09′ at 85°.

    Weather moves this very little at usable elevations. Applying Bennett's scaling to the extremes — 840 hPa at +30 °C, and the record sea-level pressure of 1083.8 hPa recorded at Agata, Siberia in 1968 at −50 °C — gives 0.77′ and 1.35′ at 45° elevation. The spread is 0.58′, which is 35 arcseconds, which is 0.0097°.

    Across the entire range of terrestrial weather, refraction at 45° elevation stays within a hundredth of a degree of one arcminute.

    What refraction actually does to the Eratosthenes measurement

    Here is the arithmetic in public, with the inputs given so a reader can redo it.

    Take Alexandria at 31.20° N and Syene (Aswan) at 24.09° N, with the obliquity of the ecliptic at about 23.72° in 240 BC. Noon solstice elevation at Alexandria is 90 − 31.20 + 23.72 = 82.52°; at Syene, 90 − 24.09 + 23.72 = 89.63°. Bennett gives 7.79″ of refraction at the first and 0.31″ at the second.

    Both stations are lifted. Only the difference touches the answer, and the difference is 7.5 arcseconds.

    The signal being measured is the difference in the two zenith angles, 31.20 − 24.09 = 7.11°, which is 25,596 arcseconds. So refraction perturbs the result by 7.48/25,596 = 0.0292 per cent — about 12 km on a 40,000 km circumference (40,075 × 0.000292 = 11.7 km).

    Set that against the measurement's own error budget. Eratosthenes reported 252,000 stadia. The stadion is known only to lie somewhere between about 155 and 160 m, which gives 39,060 km at the low end and 40,320 km at the high end — that is −2.4% to +0.8% against the 40,007.9 km polar circumference.

    Refraction is about seventy times smaller than the uncertainty the ancient measurement already carried.

    The parallel-ray assumption gets the same treatment. The astronomical unit is a defined constant, 149,597,870,700 m exactly, fixed there by IAU 2012 Resolution B2 because spacecraft ranging and planetary radar measure the distance directly by time of flight. Solar horizontal parallax follows as arctan(6,378,137/1.495978707 × 10¹¹) = 8.794 arcseconds, reproducing the standard tabulated value. Across the whole diameter of the Earth the direction to the sun therefore differs by 17.6 arcseconds.

    Two distances are in play in this section and they are not the same quantity, so we name each where we use it. The ancient report is 5,000 stadia, about 788 km at 157.5 m to the stadion; that is the figure the ancient calculation used, and it is the one we use in the degeneracy arithmetic. The modern great-circle distance between the two sites is 843 km, and that is the one we use for the parallax bound, because that bound is a statement about the actual separation of two actual points.

    Across 843 km, arctan(843 × 10³/AU) = 1.16 arcseconds. So the two optical assumptions Q12 targets are worth 7.5″ and 1.16″ against a signal of 25,596″.

    This does not show that the Earth is round. It shows only that these two corrections are not what decides it — they are three and a half orders of magnitude too small to reach the question.

    The 240 BC obliquity of 23.72° is a standard figure we did not trace to a primary ephemeris, and the latitudes of the ancient sites are approximate. We give the inputs rather than present 7.5″ as precise. A 0.1° error in the obliquity moves the differential by well under an arcsecond, which is why the conclusion survives the imprecision.

    The honest degeneracy, and the only thing that breaks it

    Now the part of Q12 that has actual force, stated as strongly as we can make it.

    Take the reported figures at face value: 788 km of ground separation and 7.2° of angular difference. On a globe, the circumference is 788 × (360/7.2) = 787.5 × 50 = 39,375 km. On a flat plane with a nearby sun, treating Syene as the subsolar point and Alexandria's sun as standing at 82.8° elevation, the sun's height is 787.5/cot(82.8°) = 787.5/0.12643 = 6,234 km.

    Both fits are exact. Two observations, two models, one dataset, no way to choose between them. Anyone who presents Eratosthenes as a proof of sphericity has claimed more than the data supports, and we would rather say so than defend it.

    Neither of those two figures is a measurement. The stadion length is uncertain by about 3%, so both 39,375 km and 6,234 km are illustrative. The structural point — that one pair fits both models — does not depend on the stadion at all.

    What resolves it is a third station, and then a fourth. Not any refinement of the original pair.

    And the discriminator turns out to be unusually clean, because the flat model's one adjustable parameter cancels out of it. Derive it. On a flat plane with the sun at height H, the ground distance x to the subsolar point satisfies tan(elevation) = H/x. At equinox the observed noon elevation is 90° − φ, where φ is the latitude as read from the sky. So x = H·tan φ, and the ground distance per degree of observed elevation change is d*x*/dφ = H·sec²φ·(π/180).

    H appears only as an overall scale. It cancels from every ratio.

    The flat-plus-near-sun model therefore requires, unavoidably and with no free parameter left, that a degree of latitude grow as sec²φ. On a spheroid the same quantity is nearly constant, growing only from flattening.

    Two models, one measurable curve, and no free parameters on either side: the predictions differ by a factor of six before you even reach the Arctic Circle, and by a factor of thirty-three at 80°.

    The measurement already exists, and it was made to settle a different argument

    The length of a degree of latitude — triangulated ground distance measured against astronomically observed zenith angles, which is exactly the quantity in dispute — was measured repeatedly in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. It was measured to settle whether the Earth is oblate, as Newton predicted, or prolate, as Cassini predicted. That dispute was entirely internal to the globe model. Neither side had any stake in the question this page is about.

    Two caveats we would rather raise ourselves. Maupertuis' Lapland figure is about 0.36% larger than the modern WGS84 value at that latitude, a discrepancy that was noticed and argued about at the time. And we take the mean latitude of Picard's Malvoisine–Amiens arc as roughly 49.4° without having verified it against the original survey. Neither caveat touches the argument. An error of 0.36% cannot rescue a prediction that is wrong by a factor of 6.19.

    The same measurement exists at modern precision. The Struve Geodetic Arc, surveyed between 1816 and 1855 through 265 station points, runs 2,821.853 ± 0.012 km and spans 25°20′08.29″ of latitude, from Fuglenes at 70°40′11.23″ N to Staro-Nekrassowka at 45°20′02.94″ N. UNESCO lists it as World Heritage site 1187, describing it as "the first accurate measuring of a long segment of a meridian." It was surveyed for imperial topographic mapping.

    And there is a check that is embedded in the units themselves. In the 1790s the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the quarter meridian, from the Delambre–Méchain survey, in service of weights-and-measures reform. The modern WGS84 quarter meridian is 10,001.966 km, so that eighteenth-century measurement of the Earth's size was off by +0.020%. The nautical mile was likewise defined as one minute of latitude, which is why the polar circumference sits very near 21,600 nautical miles.

    The quantity that separates the two models by a factor of six was measured to a fifth of a per mille, by people who were arguing about something else entirely, and then built into the definition of the metre.

    What would have to be true for shadow angles to imply a near Sun

    The sec²φ derivation applies to one specific flat model: a plane surface, a point-like sun at fixed height, and straight rays. A reader can escape it by abandoning rectilinear propagation — by proposing that the atmosphere bends the light enough to fake the observations. This section is the answer to that escape, and it is the reason the whole page is about refraction.

    Run the reconciliation. Calibrate the flat model's sun height on the equatorial arc, where the two models agree best: H = 110,577 m ÷ (π/180) = 6,336 km. Integrate the measured WGS84 degree lengths from the equator to 66.3° N and you get 7,356 km of surveyed ground. The flat model then predicts an equinox noon elevation there of arctan(6336/7356) = 40.74°.

    Every observer at the Arctic Circle measures 90 − 66.3 = 23.70°.

    The gap is 17.04 degrees.

    Now ask what the atmosphere can supply. At 23.7° apparent elevation, Bennett gives 2.24 arcminutes, which is 0.0373°. That is short by 17.04/0.0373 = a factor of 457. Grant the flat model the maximum refraction the atmosphere produces anywhere — the 34.5 arcminutes at the astronomical horizon, 0.575° — and it is still short by 17.04/0.575 = a factor of 30.

    The same invariant and the same bench-measured refractive index that make 17° impossible are what make the Eratosthenes correction 7.5 arcseconds. There is one refractive index of air, measured for reasons unrelated to any of this, and it either buys you both numbers or neither.

    Two shorter consistency checks, which need no survey at all and which we keep brief because our celestial-globes page develops them at length.

    The sun sets. With H = 6,336 km, the sun would still stand arctan(6336/20000) = 17.6° above the horizon at 20,000 km of ground distance, and arctan(6336/40000) = 9.0° at 40,000 km. On a flat plane with a finite-height sun, it never reaches the horizon at any finite distance. The available 0.575° of horizon refraction does not close a 9.0° gap. Our celestial-globes page runs the analogous argument for Polaris and puts the required bending at 1,162 arcminutes. The sun does not change size. At H = 6,336 km, a sun 10,000 km away along the ground would be √(6336² + 10000²) = 11,838 km distant rather than 6,336, and would appear 32.0 × 6336/11838 = 17.1′ across instead of 32.0′. The measured annual variation runs 31.46′ to 32.53′, a 3.4% range with no daily component, and 32.53/31.46 = 1.0340 matches the prediction from orbital eccentricity, (1+e)/(1−e) with e = 0.0167, which is also 1.0340.

    The solar-diameter figures come from an amateur photographic comparison taken six months apart, not from the Astronomical Almanac's tabulated semi-diameter, which we were unable to obtain. The exact agreement with orbital eccentricity is strong internal evidence they are right, but the citation is weaker than the rest of this page and we label it so.

    If the argument is about crepuscular rays instead

    Page 55 does not make this argument — it names shadow angles. But the variant circulates widely enough that leaving it unanswered would look like an evasion, so it is answered here.

    If the claim is that sunbeams visibly fan out from the sun and therefore come from a nearby source, the answer is perspective. The rays are parallel and appear to diverge for the same reason railway tracks do.

    The clean falsifier is anticrepuscular rays, which appear to converge towards the antisolar point — the point directly opposite the sun in the sky, 180° away. A bundle of genuinely diverging rays from a nearby source cannot also converge on the opposite point of the sky. A parallel bundle under perspective projection must produce both vanishing points. Both are observed and photographed routinely.

    We could not find a paper giving quantitative measurements of crepuscular-ray convergence angles. This section rests on the projective geometry plus the observation of anticrepuscular rays, not on a number we can hand you.

    The quantitative bound is the one already established: across the whole Earth, the direction to the sun varies by 17.6 arcseconds. The residual non-parallelism of sunlight is the sun's own angular diameter, about 32′, and that quantity is independently checked every time an eclipse is predicted. The sun's angular radius is arctan(696,000/149,597,871) = 0.2666°, so the Moon's umbral cone closes to a point 1737.4/tan(0.2666°) = 373,400 km behind it. Add the Earth's radius and you get 379,800 km, against the published total-versus-annular boundary of roughly 381,000 km from the Earth's centre — agreement to 0.42%, the residual being the difference between mean and instantaneous distances.

    That boundary is a prediction published years in advance and then checked from the ground. Eclipses with the Moon nearer than about 381,000 km are total; those farther away are annular, across a lunar distance range of 356,400 to 406,700 km. It is a continuously falsified measurement of exactly the quantity in question — how non-parallel sunlight actually is.

    Where the question has real force, and where we are not going

    Everything quantitative on this page concerns the sun well above the horizon — say above 20° of elevation. A hostile reader would say we picked the favourable geometry, and we did. We would rather state that plainly than let anyone infer from this page that refraction is small in general. It is not.

    Hirt and colleagues measured it directly, with no astronomy in the setup at all: two pairs of motorised Leica TCRP 1201 total stations on a surveyed 800 m baseline, 1.8 m above grass, one reciprocal vertical-angle reading per minute, 33 hours across five days in August and September 2008, roughly 2,000 epochs. The refraction coefficient k ran from −4 to +16 on sunny days against a textbook value of about 0.13, with fluctuations of amplitude 1 to 1.5 on ten- to thirty-minute timescales.

    The reason the two regimes differ so violently is geometric, and it falls straight out of the same formalism. The bending contributed by a layer is Δ*n*·tan z, independent of the shape of the gradient inside it. Take a 2 m surface layer with a 10 K temperature drop across it: Δ*n* = (n−1)·Δ*T*/T = 2.772 × 10⁻⁴ × 10/288 = 9.63 × 10⁻⁶. For a ray at 60° elevation, that layer adds 9.63 × 10⁻⁶ × tan 30° = 5.56 × 10⁻⁶ rad = 1.15 arcseconds. At 82.5° elevation it adds 0.26 arcseconds.

    A horizontal sight line never leaves that layer. For it one uses the ray curvature κ = (1/n)(d*n*/d*h*) = 4.81 × 10⁻⁶ per metre, and over 800 m the direction change is κ*L* = 3.85 × 10⁻³ rad = about 790 arcseconds, with an apparent target rise of κ*L*²/2 = roughly 1.5 m.

    Same air, same gradient, and six orders of magnitude difference in consequence — purely from the geometry of how long the ray stays inside the disturbed layer.

    That 10 K over 2 m gradient corresponds to k = *R*ₑ·κ = 30.7, which is beyond Hirt's observed maximum of 16, so it is an extreme case rather than a typical one. Using 2 K instead gives k = 6.1, comfortably inside his measured range, and 0.23 arcseconds at 60° elevation. The conclusion does not depend on which we pick.

    This is why we do not use horizon observations to argue about the Earth's shape, and it is the honest reason the concessions in section one are not a rhetorical courtesy. Our flat-surface-test page handles the near-horizon case directly, including the surveyor's k ≈ 0.13–0.17 and the seven-sixths-Earth rule; that page, not this one, is where the grazing-incidence concession properly lives.

    Where this page could be wrong

    Q12 does not say which shadows. It names shadow angles but not the solar elevation at which they are taken, and this page answers the noon case, where the arcsecond figures hold. If the argument is specifically about long shadows near sunrise and sunset, or about a mechanism other than refraction, large parts of this page miss their target. We have not characterised his argument beyond the two sentences he actually wrote, and we do not intend to. We chose the favourable geometry, and the choice is defensible but it is still a choice. Serious shadow-angle work is done at local noon precisely because that geometry is the well-behaved one, so restricting to it is not circular. But a reader who wanted to argue that the well-behaved regime was selected to produce a comfortable answer would be describing what we did. The horizontal-homogeneity assumption is unquantified. Every arcminute figure here assumes an atmosphere stratified horizontally. Real atmospheres have fronts, and horizontal refractive-index gradients do exist. We could not establish a measured bound on the residual from horizontal inhomogeneity at high sun elevations, and we are not going to assert it is zero. We think it very unlikely to reach degrees. "Unlikely" is not a number, and we would rather say we could not confirm it than pretend otherwise. Several inputs are secondary-sourced. The 240 BC obliquity and the ancient station latitudes are standard figures we did not trace to primary sources. The Struve arc's precise length and endpoint coordinates come from a secondary source rather than the original 1858 publication. The solar angular-diameter range is amateur photographic work rather than the Astronomical Almanac. Picard's mean arc latitude is approximate. We have flagged each at the point of use. Maupertuis was measurably off. The Lapland degree is about 0.36% larger than the modern value, and this was contested in the eighteenth century. We raise it rather than presenting the three arcs as clean agreement. The sec²φ argument does not cover every alternative. It refutes a plane with a point sun and straight rays. It says nothing about models that abandon rectilinear propagation. The 17.04° figure is the answer to that escape route, and we have presented it as such rather than as an afterthought — but a reader should understand that the two arguments do different jobs.
    We have also reused material. The plane-stratified Snell apparatus, the sign argument, the record-pressure refraction ceiling and the Polaris framing all come from our celestial-globes page, and the near-horizon treatment from our flat-surface-test page. What is new here is the Eratosthenes differential budget, the degeneracy concession, the H-free discriminator, and the 17.04° reconciliation.

    What would change our mind

    Specifically, and in advance.

    A consistent flat-model sun height across many stations. A published, replicable set of simultaneous solar-elevation measurements from four or more stations spanning at least 40° of latitude, in which the flat-plane sun height H inferred from every station pair agrees to better than 5%. Our claim is that the inferred H varies by about a factor of two across ordinary station pairs and by a factor of six or more once the span reaches the Arctic Circle. One consistent dataset would end this page. A meridian arc that grows the way the flat model needs. An independent survey showing the ground distance per degree of measured noon-sun elevation growing faster than about 1.05× between the equator and 60° latitude. The flat-plus-near-sun model needs 4.00× there. The ellipsoid predicts about 1.008×. The eighteenth-century arcs measured 1.0121× at 66.3°. A modern replication landing anywhere above 1.05 would break our reading of the existing surveys. A larger refractive index of air. A bench measurement at one atmosphere in the visible showing n − 1 larger than about 1 × 10⁻³ — roughly 3.6 times the Ciddor value. That would make the plane-stratified invariant deliver several arcminutes at 45° elevation instead of one, and we would have to redo the entire error budget. It would still leave the flat model a factor of about 100 short of the 17° it needs. A failure of the invariant itself. A demonstration that n·sin z = constant fails at sun elevations above 20° by more than the one-part-in-ten-thousand accuracy Young claims for it — for instance, a measured refraction at 45° elevation differing from (n−1)·tan z by more than a few arcseconds under stated pressure and temperature. Every quantitative claim on this page flows from that invariant. Shadow angles taken near the horizon. Q12 does not specify an elevation, and near-horizon shadows are a different regime from noon ones. If that is the claim, our concessions get considerably larger and this page needs rewriting around Hirt's k = −4 to +16 rather than around noon arcseconds. A sun that changes apparent size during the day. A measurement at a fixed site showing the solar angular diameter changing by more than about 5% between local noon and one hour before sunset. Near-sun models require roughly a factor of two over a day; the observed variation is 3.4% over a year and tracks orbital eccentricity. Circularity in the astronomical unit. Evidence that the AU as fixed by IAU 2012 Resolution B2 rests on an assumption about the Earth's shape rather than on time-of-flight ranging. If solar distance were not independently measured, the 1.16-arcsecond bound on ray non-parallelism would be circular and we would have to drop it.
    Q12's premise is correct and its instinct is sound: the optical assumption was never quantified where it should have been. We have quantified it. It comes to 7.5 arcseconds where the argument needed it to come to degrees.

    Sources & further reading

    • P. E. Ciddor, "Refractive index of air: new equations for the visible and near infrared", Applied Optics 35, 1566–1573 (1996) — the dispersion relation giving n − 1 = 2.7715 × 10⁻⁴ for standard air at 15 °C, 101.325 kPa, 450 ppm CO₂, 589 nm. Served operationally by NIST's Engineering Metrology Toolbox (emtoolbox.nist.gov/wavelength/abstract.asp) for laser interferometric length metrology, which is why the number exists at all.
    • G. G. Bennett, "The Calculation of Astronomical Refraction in Marine Navigation", Journal of Navigation 35(2), 255–259 (1982) — the refraction formula R = (0.28·P[hPa]/(T+273)) / tan(h + 7.31/(h + 4.4)), fitted to observations for sextant altitude correction. Establishes the refraction column used throughout this page.
    • A. T. Young, "The Plane-parallel Model", aty.sdsu.edu/explain/atmos_refr/models/flat.html — states the n·sin z = constant invariant, the resulting approximation r = (n−1)·tan z, and its accuracy of better than one part in ten thousand while r stays under about half a degree.
    • S. Y. van der Werf and W. H. Lehn, "Atmospheric refraction: a history", Applied Optics 44(27), 5624 (2005) — establishes that the first accurate measurements of atmospheric refraction were made by Tycho Brahe in the sixteenth century. We quote the abstract only; we did not obtain the full paper and quote none of Brahe's tabulated values.
    • C. Hirt, S. Guillaume, A. Wisbar, B. Bürki and H. Sternberg, "Monitoring of the refraction coefficient in the lower atmosphere using a controlled setup of simultaneous reciprocal vertical angle measurements", J. Geophys. Res. Atmospheres, 2010JD014067 — 800 m baseline, 1.8 m above grass, 33 hours, ~2,000 epochs; measured refraction coefficient k from −4 to +16 against a textbook 0.13. Establishes the near-ground concession.
    • W. B. Herrmannsfeldt, "SLAC Alignment System", proceedings.jacow.org/l66/papers/iv-05.pdf — the two-mile linac aligned through a 24-inch evacuated pipe at 10–25 torr to 0.025 mm, "to avoid distortion and translation of the image due to refraction of the light in the atmosphere". Establishes that engineers remove air from long horizontal sight lines. We found no statement in this paper about the linac's axis versus Earth's curvature and make no claim about it.
    • Cleomedes, Caelestia i.7.49–52 — the surviving simplified account of Eratosthenes' measurement (his own On the measure of the Earth is lost): 5,000 stadia, 7.2°, 252,000 stadia. Modern assessment of the stadion length via the standard treatments; establishes the −2.4% to +0.8% error bar.
    • Picard (Paris–Amiens, 1669), Bouguer and La Condamine (French Geodesic Mission to the Equator, 1735–44), and Maupertuis (Torne valley, Lapland, 1736–37; La Figure de la Terre, 1738) — degree-of-latitude measurements of 57,060, 56,734 and 57,422 toises, made to settle whether the Earth is oblate or prolate. Establishes the measured latitude dependence of the degree, from a dispute with no stake in the present one.
    • UNESCO World Heritage listing 1187, whc.unesco.org/en/list/1187 — the Struve Geodetic Arc, 1816–1855, over 2,820 km, "the first accurate measuring of a long segment of a meridian". Precise length (2,821.853 ± 0.012 km), station count and endpoint coordinates are secondary-sourced and not traced to the original 1858 publication.
    • WGS84 ellipsoid parameters (a = 6,378,137 m, f = 1/298.257223563) — establishes the modern degree lengths, the 10,001.966 km quarter meridian, and the 40,007.9 km polar circumference against which the historical figures are checked.
    • IAU 2012 Resolution B2, syrte.obspm.fr/IAU_resolutions/Res_IAU2012_B2.pdf — fixes the astronomical unit at 149,597,870,700 m exactly, on the stated grounds that modern range measurements make distance ratios unnecessary. Establishes solar horizontal parallax of 8.794″ without reference to the Earth's shape.
    • ASTM G173 reference solar spectra as published by NREL — "An absolute air mass of 1.5 (solar zenith angle 48.19°)". Establishes the photovoltaic industry's working figure for how much atmosphere sunlight crosses.
    • Les Cowley, Atmospheric Optics, atoptics.co.uk/blog/crepuscular-rays-and-shadows — anticrepuscular rays "appear to converge towards the antisolar point, which is the point opposite to the position of the sun in the sky". Establishes the perspective explanation qualitatively; we found no paper giving quantitative convergence angles.
    • University of Waterloo CEMC, cemc.uwaterloo.ca/about-us/news/demystifying-solar-eclipses — solar radius 696,000 km, lunar distance range 356,400–406,700 km, and the total-versus-annular boundary near 381,000 km. Establishes the eclipse cross-check on the Sun's angular size.
    • Photographic comparison of solar apparent diameter at aphelion and perihelion, perseus.gr/Astro-Solar-Scenes-Aph-Perihelion.htm (TeleVue Pronto, Canon EOS 300D, Baader filter, January and July 2005) — 31.46′ to 32.53′. Amateur single-observer source, flagged as such; the Astronomical Almanac's tabulated semi-diameter would be the stronger citation and we could not obtain it.
    • Levi Miller, Globe Deconstruction? prerelease draft, p. 55 — the text of Q12, quoted verbatim at the head of this page.
    • Companion pages: funwithscience.net/globe-deconstruction/celestial-globes/ (the plane-stratified Snell apparatus, the 0 °C bench index, the Agata 1083.8 hPa refraction ceiling, the 1,162-arcminute Polaris framing) and funwithscience.net/globe-deconstruction/flat-surface-test/ (surveyor's k ≈ 0.13–0.17, the seven-sixths-Earth rule, the near-horizon case).
