The Equator Flight Data Challenge, Answered

A good experiment, the right route, and a table that computes the flat answer with the globe's own numbers.

The chapter opening at p. 117 asks for something the rest of the book has been circling: a test with no refraction in it, no eyesight, no photographs — “just raw data and numbers” (p. 118). Fly a private jet along the equator, log the flight recorder, count the steering inputs. A globe needs none; a flat Earth needs a hundred and nineteen. That is a real experiment with a real discriminator, and it deserves an answer rather than a dismissal.

Good experimentTable is inconsistentThe short version

He picked the sharpest route on Earth. But the 119° in his table is 8,250 miles converted into degrees using the globe's circumference — and p. 120 declines to give the flat Earth a scale at all, so the flat side of his own comparison has no number in it. It does not need one. Three figures settle it, and none depends on any map:

The rest of this page is why those three numbers are what they are, why the aeroplane's own instruments mostly will not show them, and why the measurement has already been made several million times.

1 · The challenge, stated fairly

“What if we took a private jet and flew directly over the coordinates of the equator? … If the Earth is truly a globe, we should be able to fly in a straight line to reach the other side of the world going east or west! We should see over 100 steering adjustments to maintain the course on a flat Earth, with opposite adjustments on the return trip.”
— Levi Miller, Globe Deconstruction, p. 121 (review draft)

The setup as printed: Quito to Pontianak along the equator, 8,250 miles at 560 mph, 14.73 hours, 119° of turn at 8.08°/hr, one steering input every 7.4 minutes, $715,000.

Four things here are right, and they should be said first.

  • — The experiment is sound. Turn rate is physical, it is recorded on every flight, and the two models genuinely disagree about it. This is a better argument than anything in the book resting on eyesight.
  • — The globe's prediction is exactly what he says. The equator is a great circle; flying it requires no turn at all. Not “a small turn” — zero, identically.
  • — And he picked the right quantity. An aeroplane is not turned by the air. Lift acts perpendicular to the wings; to turn, you bank. So the flat model's requirement is not a residual buried in a position log, it is a commanded action — a persistent one-directional bank, reversing on the return leg. Recorders log actions.
  • — And the analysis on p. 119 is correct. The attitude indicator's pendulous vanes erect the gyro to local vertical, identically whether that vertical is normal to a plane or radial to a globe. “No shape debate points for either side” is right, and it is more careful than either camp usually manages. It comes back in §5, doing more work than he realised. Though the two jets he names carry no vane-erected instrument — a G800 or Global 8000 computes attitude in a laser inertial reference, which as §6 shows makes the modern “level” reference itself part of the Earth model.

2 · The number in the table

One definition, because everything below rests on it. “Turn” here means yaw about the local vertical — what a turn coordinator shows and a rate gyro senses. Fly right round the equator and your nose does swing through a full circle, but about the north–south axis; that is the nose-down rotation of §5, not a turn.

Where does 119° come from? Not the longitude span of the named route: Mariscal Sucre (UIO) is at 78.36°W and Supadio (PNK) at 109.40°E, which is 187.8° apart going east and 172.2° going west — about 13,000 and 11,900 miles, not 8,250. The airports and the distance are not describing the same flight. The 119° comes from the distance instead:

8,250 mi ÷ 24,901 mi × 360° = 119.3°

24,901 miles is the circumference of the equator on a globe. The figure presented as the flat model's turning requirement was obtained by measuring the route on the globe.

Which raises the question the chapter has to answer before it can quote a flat figure at all: how long is the equator on a flat Earth? p. 120 declines to say. The Gleason map is dismissed there as “just a distorted version of the globe,” the ice wall as imaginary, measured distances as possibly “elongated to conform to a predefined Earth radius.” What survives is a requirement rather than a map — that circumnavigation grows past the equator. That is a more careful position than most flat-Earth writing, and it means the flat side of this comparison has no scale, so no distance, so no angle.

And the experiment does not need one. Take any flat map you like — any size, any projection, any outline — and draw the equator on it. It is a closed loop with the pole inside. Fly right round any closed loop in a plane and you return pointing the way you started, having turned through exactly 360°, whatever its size or shape. On a globe the equator is a great circle: exactly 0°. The gap is one full revolution and no choice of map can move it.

That is the Gauss–Bonnet theorem — turning supplied by the traveller, plus curvature enclosed by the circuit, always sums to 2π. A plane supplies none of it so the pilot supplies all; a globe supplies all so the pilot supplies none. Every disputed quantity — map scales, road lengths, who controls the tracking data — drops straight out. One exit closed: some flat maps are bipolar or rectangular, and on those the equator is not a loop, so eastbound circumnavigation is impossible — refuted by the Whitbread voyage the chapter itself cites on p. 121.

If a specific map is wanted for the partial leg, take the only concrete layout consistent with his own requirement: parallels as circles about the pole, spaced by true north–south distance. On that arrangement the equator is a circle of radius 6,225 miles, so 39,115 miles around against the globe's 24,901 — a ratio of exactly π/2.

The table's 8.08°/hr is 119° divided by 14.73 hours — both inputs globe measurements, the answer used as a flat prediction. Run consistently, the same flight needs 76 inputs, not 119. That does not break the experiment: anything that can tell 119° from 0° can tell 76° from 0°. It changes the number to look for.

3 · Why the equator, and what “turning” means

Three separate things make this the sharpest route on the planet, and the chapter states none of them — which is worth doing on its behalf, because together they are a point in its favour.

  • 1. It is the only line of latitude that is also a great circle. Everywhere else, “hold this parallel” and “don't steer” are two different instructions and an aircraft can obey only one. At the equator they are the same instruction, so both models can be asked about the same manoeuvre.
  • 2. The geometric requirement is exactly zero there. Holding a parallel on a globe needs a turn rate of (V÷R) tan φ, and tan 0° = 0. The flat map needs V÷r, which is never zero anywhere.
  • 3. And the lateral Coriolis deflection vanishes there too. It goes as sin φ. Flying east at the equator the Coriolis term points straight up — the Eötvös effect, 0.37% of g — and does not steer you at all. Take the same wings-level aeroplane to 45°N and its track curves at 21.3°/hr from Coriolis alone, a hundred miles in the first hour. The equator is the only place on Earth where an aircraft can be left alone and still be doing something meaningful.

“But you can just drive straight west without turning”

This is the objection to raise here, and it is the right one. Hold a heading of 270°: the compass does not move, nothing says turn. How can that be a turn?

You can indeed drive perfectly straight. You just stop going west when you do. Straight and due west are different instructions, and on a sphere they come apart. Three ways of seeing it, because asserting it convinces nobody.

Turn the numbers up. Stand ten metres from the North Pole and walk due west. You walk a circle twenty metres across, a lap every forty-five seconds, turning at eight degrees per second — and your compass reads 270° throughout. Nobody would say you had not turned. Walk south and the circle grows and the turn gentles — 30°/hr at 75°N, 8.1°/hr at 45°N, 2.2°/hr at 15°N — until at the equator it reaches exactly zero.

Drive it. Hold the wheel dead centre and the straight line peels away from due west toward the equator: 6.7 inches in a mile at 40°N, 20 feet across a six-mile township, 724 feet in thirty-six miles.

And American surveyors have been correcting for that on the ground for well over a century. The public-land township grid runs its standard parallels along lines of latitude, and because a parallel is not straight a crew cannot sight a transit and walk — they lay out a curve. The field tables carry dedicated tables for it: Offsets from the tangent to the parallel, and a companion for the secant method. Printed, carried into the field, used — because a township line that ignores the correction closes about twenty feet out and the corners do not meet. Offsets computed here from tan φ ÷ R rather than read from the tables; the grid dates to 1785, and the 1910 edition is the one cited.

Your instinct is right about everything a pilot or driver would feel, though. At 45°N and 560 mph the bank needed to hold the parallel is 3.4 arcminutes, and nobody commands it — you command a heading, and the turn is what holding that heading means on a sphere. So both intuitions are correct about different things: “I am not turning” is right about the controls and the compass; “you are turning” is right about the path. The arbiter is a gyro, which holds against space rather than against a reference quietly rotating underneath it.

This distinction defeats most people who think the Earth is round, and the usual reply to this chapter — “the equator is a great circle, so no turn is needed” — is true but answers a question nobody asked. It never explains why the pilot's experience feels identical at every latitude. A challenge that opens a hole that wide in the standard defence is not a cheap one.

If the framing still feels like a definitional trick, close the circuit and it stops being one. Go right round a parallel and come back facing the way you started; something rotated through a full turn, and the only question is how much of it you supplied:

The globe column is 360° × sin φ and the gap is the curvature enclosed — the share of the turning the planet does for you. At the pole you circle a point, supply all of it, and the models agree exactly. At the equator the planet supplies all of it and they disagree by a full revolution.

Although nobody flies a parallel anyway

Holding a line of latitude is a real manoeuvre with a real turn rate, and it is also something no airline has ever asked a crew to do. On a globe the shortest path is a great circle, which bows toward the pole — London to Tokyo peaks at 70.9°N and runs 5,946 miles against 7,027 on a constant heading, eighteen per cent shorter. New York to Beijing peaks at 83.9°N and saves thirty-one per cent. That is not a detour; it is the short way, flown daily with the fuel bill to prove it.

Which is worth knowing before reading too much into the northern rows of the chart. Compare the models on the route aircraft actually fly — the shortest one — and they nearly agree: the flat map's best London–Tokyo path peaks at 74.6°N against the globe's 70.9°N. A few hundred miles of cross-track, real but not obvious from a window. The northern hemisphere has never been where this map gets into trouble, and the chapter is right not to look for the argument there.

4 · What you would, and would not, actually see

Here the challenge gets more interesting than either side usually allows. 5.15°/hr sounds large because it is quoted per hour; spread across a flight it is not something a pilot would feel or see. Instrument by instrument, at the equator, eastbound:

The heading row is the subtle one and the place a lazy rebuttal goes wrong. On the flat map “north” means toward the centre, so flying the equator circle keeps the pole exactly abeam and the heading indicator sits on 090° — the same reading a globe gives. Magnetic heading alone cannot separate the models. True heading throughout: declination shifts from about 4°W at Quito to near zero at Pontianak, but that is a property of the magnetic field, identical under either model.

A gyroscope does not know where the pole is. It holds its orientation against inertial space, which is why a directional gyro drifts against the compass and why pilots reset it. That drift is a measurement: earth rate, 15.04°/hr × sin φ, plus a transport term for the aircraft's own motion. At the equator flying east both are exactly zero. The globe says gyro and compass stay locked for the whole fourteen hours; the flat map says they separate by 76°.
But the certified black box will not show it, and we should say so. Under 14 CFR Part 121 Appendix M, roll attitude is mandated at 0.5° resolution and ±2° accuracy, once a second. The bank in question is 0.04° — twelve times below the resolution and fifty times below the accuracy. Raw gyro body rates are not mandated at all, and recorded heading comes from the inertial reference after earth-rate and transport corrections, so it reads 090° either way. Pull the FDR off an equator flight and the divergence is not in the file.

So the honest answer is narrower than “check the black box.” What works is raw inertial-reference output or a quick-access recorder logging body rates — which needs the operator's cooperation — or, more simply, bringing your own gyro: navigation-grade (0.01°/hr) rented, or tactical-grade at 0.5–1°/hr bought for a few thousand and calibrated stationary before and after.

One thing to be clear about, because it is the obvious way to get a wrong answer: a gyro in a seat does not read zero on a globe. It reads earth rate, plus transport, plus every genuine heading change at every waypoint. The comparison that means anything is integrated gyro yaw against heading change derived from the GPS track — and the globe predicts that difference to be the integral of (15.04 sin φ + V tan φ ÷ R), which is zero only on the equator. The flat map predicts the integral of V÷r, which is never zero anywhere.

5 · The island of pilots

The chapter closes with a good rhetorical move. Imagine pilots who have never seen a globe. Introduce the model and they say: “But we never point our nose down to go around the curve, and there is no adjustment for Earth spin. That's impossible.”

The rate at which the nose would have to drop is V÷R — at 560 mph, 8.1° per hour, a seventh of a degree per minute. But nobody performs it.

The mechanism is the one he described correctly himself, two pages earlier. The pendulous vanes erect the attitude indicator to local vertical, continuously. On a globe local vertical rotates as you travel, at exactly V÷R. An aircraft flown level against that indicator is following the curve automatically, with nobody pointing anything anywhere. His own analysis of the instrument is why the island pilots never feel what they are being asked to feel.

And the objection has the two directions the wrong way round. Vertically the aeroplane is held on the curve by an equilibrium with a restoring force: it flies a constant pressure altitude, and an isobaric surface wraps the planet. Try to fly a Euclidean straight line and you climb off it, lose lift and settle back. Level flight is curved flight, and it is free. Horizontally there is no aerodynamic mechanism at all — air does not push an aeroplane sideways, and whatever horizontal curvature exists comes from bank, or away from the equator from Coriolis. So the flat model needs the kind of curve that cannot happen by itself, sustained for fourteen hours, while the globe needs the kind that cannot be avoided.

Which suggests a cleaner version of the experiment

Fly the equator eastbound and take the steering out of the loop: roll-attitude hold at zero bank — an autopilot wings-level mode, or a pilot flying zero bank against the attitude indicator — with no heading reference and no lateral navigation engaged. Not “let go of everything”: a transport left entirely alone rolls off into its spiral mode, and persistent roll trim from rigging or fuel imbalance is anyway indistinguishable from a 0.04° bank. Hold the wings level, steer nothing.

One confound has to be handled, and it is serious. A crosswind of just 22 knots puts you 25 miles off track in an hour — the flat map's entire first-hour prediction, produced by ordinary weather. Two things separate them, and both come from the same log. Shape: wind drift accumulates linearly, while the flat departure grows as the square of distance — 25, 100, 391. What moves: wind changes track relative to heading, which is drift angle and which the FMS already displays along with the wind vector; the flat map instead moves the pole-referenced heading itself at 5.15°/hr with the yaw rate at zero.

With the wind accounted for, this is still the version needing no arcminutes. A hundred miles off track after two hours is a GPS readout, a radar fix, a coastline, a noon sun-line — four orders of magnitude larger than the bank angle in §4, arriving as a position, with the pilot taken out of the loop. Worth flying as a one-hour segment of any scheduled positioning sector.

6 · Every flight is the experiment, not just this one

The chapter budgets $715,000 and hopes for a sponsor. It does not need one, and the reason is stronger than “airliners fly and seem fine.” A flight recorder does not merely log where the aeroplane ended up; it logs a closed loop, and a loop is much harder to fake than a destination. What the aircraft was told to do; what it did, measured by gyros and accelerometers against inertial space rather than against any map; and where it ended up, cross-checked against GPS and ground stations.

Those three have to reconcile, and what reconciles them is a model of the Earth's shape. To get from “the gyros turned through this much” to “so we are here,” the navigation computer needs the planet's radius, rotation rate and gravity field, and they sit in the mechanisation as explicit constants. Wrong shape and the loop does not close — the aeroplane arrives somewhere other than where its own instruments said, on every flight, in a direction that depends on latitude.

The log is an equation, and the shape of the Earth is one of its terms. Any flight supplies enough of the others to solve for it, which is why the route does not matter — Quito to Pontianak, Heathrow to Newark, a positioning leg round the pattern. What the equator buys is not validity but elegance: three terms go to zero at once, so the equation stops comparing two numbers and starts comparing a number with nothing. His instinct about the route was right; only the arithmetic needed fixing.

And the terms in that mechanisation are the ones §3 computed:

Which answers the island pilots better than §5 did. “There is no adjustment for Earth spin” — there is, it is the first row, applied several hundred times a second on every aircraft carrying an inertial reference. Pilots never see it because it happens inside a box the size of a shoebox, not because it is absent.

And the loop is checked to about a mile an hour: a navigation-grade system dead-reckons with roughly a nautical mile per hour of drift, some fifteen nautical miles across a fourteen-hour flight, watched against GPS the whole way.

The box measures the size of the Earth, with no windows

An inertial platform has to be Schuler tuned: its error loop is set so the instrument behaves like a pendulum whose length is the Earth's radius. That gives a characteristic period,

T = 2π √(R ÷ g) = 84.4 minutes

and it is the period at which every inertial navigator's error is observed to oscillate. Solve backwards for R and out comes 6,370 kilometres.

A sealed instrument, bolted in a bay, with no view of the sky and no reference to anything outside the aircraft, returns the radius of the Earth to three significant figures. Not read off a dial — it is the value that must be in the loop for the platform to hold vertical at all.

Mechanise it with the wrong radius and the platform does not become unstable; it acquires a tilt that grows with distance flown, because the rate at which the navigator commands the platform to rotate no longer matches the rate at which local vertical actually rotates. The mismatch is V × (1/Rtrue − 1/Rassumed). Put the flat map's 6,225 miles in place of 3,963 and after one hour at 560 mph the platform is tilted by 560 × (1/3,963 − 1/6,225) = 0.051 rad, 2.9° off vertical — twice that after two hours — so the accelerometers read about 0.05 g of horizontal acceleration that is not there and integrate it twice into position. Hundreds of miles in the first hour, as a mechanism rather than a percentage.

And the alignment before pushback is a second, independent check. The unit sits still, senses the Earth's rotation vector at 15.04°/hr, and resolves it into horizontal and vertical components: the horizontal one points at true north, and the ratio gives latitude directly, checked against the position the crew typed in. That procedure assumes the rotation vector is fixed in space and that its angle to local vertical is the co-latitude — tested at every airport, at every latitude, tens of thousands of times a day. It also degrades exactly where the geometry says: north-finding depends on the horizontal component, which goes as cos φ, so alignments lengthen toward the poles and eventually will not complete. Gyrotheodolites become unusable within roughly 15° of the pole for the same reason — see the rotation page — and neither limit has any meaning on a plane.

7 · The follow-up experiment, which is the better one

The chapter does not stop at the equator. It proposes a second flight, and this one goes almost unremarked: “start from the bottom of India, Africa, or Australia and fly a route that perfectly follows magnetic South.” That is the sharper test of the two, because southbound the models disagree not about a rate but about a topology: on a globe those tracks converge to a point and cross to the other side, while on any pole-centred flat map they spread apart without limit and never meet.

But the obvious evidence is the wrong evidence. The famous Antarctic flights — Hi Fly's A340 into Wolf's Fang, Norse's 787 into Troll, the Australian charters — run almost straight south, which is precisely the direction an azimuthal-equidistant chart is designed to get right: it preserves distance along its radii by construction. Cape Town to Troll is 16° off a meridian so it does differ — 2,700 miles against 3,800 — but that is a weak test dressed as a strong one.

What discriminates is any leg with a substantial east–west component down there, where the map's circumference has to stretch:

The last is the one to keep. Novolazarevskaya sits at 11.8°E and Progress at 76.4°E, both around 70°S — an east–west hop between two research stations, flown by Basler BT-67 in a working day on the DROMLAN network. On a pole-centred flat map those stations lie on a circle 44,000 miles around and the hop becomes 11,800 miles: a fortnight's flying for an aircraft with under 2,000 miles of range. It is flown routinely, so the map is wrong by a factor of eight.

And the transpolar version has been flown commercially, with passengers and a published schedule. Pan Am Flight 50, 28–30 October 1977, a 747SP named Clipper New Horizons: San Francisco over the North Pole to London, London to Cape Town, then Cape Town to Auckland across the South Pole, and home — the whole circuit in 54 hours 7 minutes. That third leg reaches 74°S and runs 7,300 miles on a globe; on the flat map it is 17,000, beyond the range of any aircraft ever built.

One clarification on his wording: a route following magnetic south leads to the south magnetic pole at roughly 64°S 136°E, off Adélie Land — a real place in either model, so the compass heading is not itself the discriminator. What discriminates is whether routes running south from different continents draw together or spread apart.

8 · What would settle it

  • 1. A gyro on a table. A navigation-grade gyro, stationary, measures earth rate about the local vertical: 15.04°/hr × sin φ. Take it to two latitudes a few hundred miles apart and the reading changes by a predictable amount. No aircraft, no charter, no permits — the same measurement as the surveying test on the self-test page.
  • 2. The wings-level leg. Zero bank over the equator, no heading or lateral-nav reference, logging position and wind. The globe says you stay on the line; the flat map says 100 miles off in two hours, growing as the square of distance rather than linearly the way wind drift does.
  • 3. The full route, if somebody funds it. Worth doing, and it would produce a public dataset. The prediction to record is accumulated yaw of the inertial platform relative to the local horizontal frame, eastbound along the equator: the globe says 0.0° to within instrument bias, the flat map 75.9° over 8,250 miles.

The honest bottom line

The chapter asks for raw data and numbers, and it is entitled to them. The numbers say the experiment was well chosen, that the equator is the right route for the reasons he intuited and two more he did not state, and that his reading of the attitude indicator is correct. They also say the table's 119° was obtained with the globe's own circumference, and that the flat model as the chapter deliberately leaves it supplies no scale from which a rival figure could be computed.

None of which sinks the experiment, because the strongest form of it never needed a scale. One circuit of the equator is 360° of turning on any flat surface and 0° on a globe, and the difference is the curvature itself. The aeroplane is not the instrument that settles it — the gyro is, and it settled it decades ago, on every route rather than this one.

He wanted raw numbers from a black box. There is a black box on every aircraft in the sky; it has been logging exactly those numbers since the first jets crossed an ocean without a navigator, and one of the numbers it quietly reports — from a sealed case with no view of anything — is that the Earth is 6,370 km in radius.
