The Equator Flight Data Challenge, Answered

A good experiment, the right route, and a table that computes the flat answer with the globe's own numbers.

The chapter opening at p. 117 asks for something the rest of the book has been circling: an experiment with no refraction in it, no eyesight, no photographs — “just raw data and numbers” (p. 118). Fly a private jet along the equator, log the flight recorder, and count the steering inputs. A globe needs none. A flat Earth needs a hundred and nineteen. That is a real test with a real discriminator, and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal. This page gives one: the experiment is sound, the route is the best one available, one of the chapter's technical passages is simply correct — and the number in the table cannot be produced by either model.

Good experimentTable is inconsistentThe short version

The equator is the sharpest test on Earth, and the cleanest version of it needs no map and no distances: fly all the way round any closed loop on any flat surface and you turn through exactly 360°; go round the equator on a globe and you turn through exactly 0°. The chapter's 119°, though, comes from converting 8,250 miles into degrees with the globe's circumference — and p. 120 declines to give the flat Earth a scale at all, so the flat side of its own comparison has no number in it. The experiment survives the correction, but it did not need funding either: every flight already runs it, because a recorder logs a closed loop — commands, measured rotations, resulting track — and only one planet closes it. And there is a version needing no instruments at all: trim the aircraft over the equator and stop steering. A globe keeps you on the line, because it is the one latitude where both the geometric turn requirement and the Coriolis deflection vanish. The flat map puts you 100 miles off it in two hours.

1 · The challenge, stated fairly

“What if we took a private jet and flew directly over the coordinates of the equator? … If the Earth is truly a globe, we should be able to fly in a straight line to reach the other side of the world going east or west! We should see over 100 steering adjustments to maintain the course on a flat Earth, with opposite adjustments on the return trip.”
— Levi Miller, Globe Deconstruction, p. 121 (review draft)

The proposed setup, as printed:

Distance8,250 miles

Degrees of turn119°
Rate of turn8.08° per hour
Steering inputs119, one every 7.4 minutes
Budget$715,000

Several things here are right, and they should be said before anything else — including one that most replies to this chapter would miss.

  • — The experiment is sound. Turn rate is a physical quantity, it is recorded on every flight, and the two models genuinely disagree about it. This is a better argument than anything in the book that depends on eyesight, cameras or photographs.
  • — The globe's prediction is exactly what he says it is. The equator is a great circle. Flying it requires no turn at all — not “a small turn,” not “a turn within tolerance.” Zero, identically.
  • — And the route is the best possible choice, for a reason the chapter does not state but which is worth spelling out, because it is a point in his favour. On every other line of latitude the two models both demand a turn, and above about 60° they demand nearly the same turn. The equator is the one place on Earth where one model says a number and the other says zero. Of all the routes he could have picked, this is the one that discriminates hardest.
  • — And the quantity he picked is the right one, for a reason worth spelling out. An aeroplane is not turned by the air. Lift acts perpendicular to the wings; to turn, you bank and point a component of it sideways. Nothing in the airflow will do that on its own, and there is no equilibrium the aircraft settles into that produces a sustained horizontal curve. So the flat model's requirement is not a subtle residual buried in a position log — it is a commanded input: a persistent, one-directional bank held for fourteen hours, reversing on the return leg. That is a far better thing to hunt for than a distance or a coordinate, because it is an action, and the recorder logs actions. His instinct that the flight recorder is the right instrument is sound.
  • — And the analysis on p. 119 is correct. The attitude indicator's pendulous vanes erect the gyro to local vertical, and they do it identically whether that vertical is normal to a flat plane or radial to a globe. His conclusion — “no shape debate points for either side” — is right, and it is a more careful piece of reasoning than either camp usually manages on the subject. Worth noting in passing that the two jets he names carry no vane-erected instrument: attitude on a G800 or a Global 8000 comes from a laser inertial reference whose vertical is computed, not sensed — which, as §6 shows, makes the modern “level” reference itself a piece of the Earth model. Hold on to it, because it comes back in §5 and it does more work than he realised.

2 · The number in the table

Start with the conversion, because everything downstream depends on it. Where does 119° come from? It is not the longitude span of the named route. Mariscal Sucre (UIO) sits at 78.36°W and Supadio (PNK) at 109.40°E, which is 187.8° apart going east and 172.2° going west — about 13,000 and 11,900 miles respectively, not 8,250. So the airports and the distance in the table are not describing the same flight. Both fields are also about nine miles south of the equator, which is a detail the route would have to deal with rather than a problem with it.

The 119° comes from the distance instead:

8,250 mi ÷ 24,901 mi × 360° = 119.3°

24,901 miles is the circumference of the equator on a globe. That is the step to look at. The figure being presented as the flat model's turning requirement has been obtained by measuring the route on the globe.

One definition first, because everything below depends on it. “Turn” here means yaw about the local vertical — rotation about the axis pointing at your own feet. That is the quantity a turn coordinator shows, a rate gyro senses and an inertial reference integrates into heading. It is not the same as the aircraft's rotation in space: fly all the way round the equator and your nose does swing through a full circle, but about the north–south axis, which is the nose-down rotation of §5, not a turn. Every 0° and 360° on this page is yaw about local vertical.

Which raises a question the chapter has to answer before it can quote a flat-Earth figure at all: how long is the equator on a flat Earth? And p. 120 declines to say. The Gleason map is dismissed there as “just a distorted version of the globe,” the ice wall as imaginary, and measured distances generally as possibly “elongated to conform to a predefined Earth radius.” What survives is a requirement rather than a map: that circumnavigation gets longer past the equator.

That is a fair position to hold, and it is more careful than most flat-Earth writing. But it means the flat side of this comparison has no number in it. There is no scale, so there is no distance, so there is no angle. The 119° had to come from somewhere, and the only geometry on the table with a scale attached was the globe's.

And here is the part that makes the experiment survive anyway: it does not need the scale. Take any flat map you like — any size, any projection, any shape of outline — and draw the equator on it. It is a closed loop with the pole inside it. Fly all the way round any closed loop in a plane and you come back pointing the way you started, having turned through exactly 360°. Not approximately, and not depending on the loop's size or shape. On a globe the equator is a great circle, and going all the way round it you turn through exactly 0°. The gap between the two models is one full revolution, and no choice of map, scale, or distance data can move it.

The missing 360° is not an artefact of anything. It is the enclosed curvature, by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem — the turning a traveller supplies round a closed circuit, plus the integrated curvature of the surface enclosed, always sums to 2π. A plane supplies none of it, so the pilot supplies all of it. A globe supplies all of it, so the pilot supplies none. That is what the experiment is actually weighing, and it is why the disputed quantities — map scales, road lengths, who controls the flight-tracking data — drop out of it entirely.

One exit to close before moving on. That argument assumes the equator is a closed loop on the flat map, which it is on any pole-centred layout — and p. 120's own requirement, that circumnavigation grows past the equator, forces a pole-centred layout. Some flat maps in circulation are bipolar or rectangular instead, and on those the equator is not a loop at all, so an aircraft flying east can never return to where it began. That is refuted by every eastbound round-the-world flight, and by the Whitbread circumnavigation the chapter itself cites on p. 121 — Hervé sailed round, and coming back to your start is the one thing a non-closed equator cannot allow.

If a specific map is wanted

For the partial leg in the table a scale is needed, so take the only concrete layout consistent with the chapter's own requirement: parallels as circles about the pole, spaced by true north–south distance. That is the azimuthal-equidistant arrangement — the Gleason map's geometry, whatever one thinks of Gleason's chart. On it the equator is a circle of radius 6,225 miles, so it is 39,115 miles around against the globe's 24,901. The ratio is exactly π/2.

That 57% is worth pausing on, because it sounds larger than people expect at the equator. It is not a special feature of the equator; it is one point on a curve that starts at nothing under the pole and runs away to infinity at the far rim:

The table's 8.08°/hr is 119° divided by 14.73 hours. Both inputs to that division are globe measurements; the answer is being used as a flat-model prediction. Run consistently on the flat map, the same flight needs 75.9° of accumulated turn at 5.15°/hr — 76 inputs, not 119.

This does not break the experiment, and it is worth being clear about that. A 36% error in the predicted turn count would matter enormously if the globe's prediction were, say, 60°. It is zero. Any experiment that can tell 119° from 0° can tell 76° from 0° just as easily. The correction changes the number to look for; it does not weaken the test. If the flight is ever flown, 76° is the figure to check against.

There is a second thing worth noticing about the conversion, and it is not arithmetic. Pages 118 to 120 argue that map distances are unreliable — that roads measure longer than Google represents them, that southern-hemisphere distances may be “elongated to conform to a predefined Earth radius,” that navigation systems may have been manipulated. Four pages later the experiment's central number is derived by trusting the globe's distance scale to four significant figures. Both positions cannot be held at once, and the chapter needs the second one to generate its headline figure.

3 · What the two models actually predict

Two results, and they do different jobs. The first needs no map at all: a full circumnavigation of the equator is 360° of accumulated turn on any flat surface and 0° on a globe. That one is safe from every argument about scales and distances. The second is the rate at each latitude, which does need a specific map, and is given below on the azimuthal-equidistant layout. It is worth having because it explains why the equator is the best route and why nothing else would have worked as well.

On a globe, holding a line of latitude at speed V needs a turn rate of (V÷R) × tan φ. The tangent is the whole story: it is zero at the equator, and it climbs steeply toward the poles. On the flat map, a parallel is a circle of radius 69.17 × (90 − φ) miles about the pole, so holding it needs V ÷ r — which is never zero anywhere, because the circle never straightens out.

“But you can just drive straight west without turning”

This is the objection to raise here, and it is the right one. Hold a heading of 270°. The compass does not move. Nothing in the cockpit — or the cab — says turn. How can that possibly be a turn?

You can indeed drive perfectly straight. You just stop going west when you do. Straight and due west are two different instructions, and on a sphere they come apart. That is the whole of it, and it is worth seeing three ways, because it is the least intuitive thing on this page and simply asserting it convinces nobody.

First, turn the numbers up. Stand ten metres from the North Pole and walk due west. You walk a circle twenty metres across — ten out to the pole and ten back. You complete a lap every forty-five seconds, turning at eight degrees per second, and your compass reads 270° the entire time. Nobody watching would say you had not turned. That is the same manoeuvre as flying west at 45°N; only the dial has been turned up.

Walk south and the circle grows and the turn gentles — 30°/hr at 75°N, 8.1°/hr at 45°N, 2.2°/hr at 15°N — until at the equator the circle finally becomes a great circle and the turn reaches exactly zero. That is what the tan φ is doing: infinite at the pole, nothing at the equator.

Second, drive it. Hold the wheel dead centre and you follow the straight line, which peels away from due west toward the equator. The gap is small, but it is not abstract and it is not invisible:

Third, and best: American surveyors have been correcting for this on the ground for well over a century. The public-land township grid — laid out from the Land Ordinance of 1785 onward — runs its standard parallels along lines of latitude — and because a parallel is not a straight line, a crew cannot simply sight a transit and walk. They have to lay out a curve. The field tables issued to public-land surveyors carry dedicated tables for exactly this: Offsets from the tangent to the parallel, and a companion table for the secant method, which splits the error either side instead. Run a great circle, then step back onto the parallel by the tabulated amount at each half-mile. Those tables were printed, carried into the field and used, because a township line that ignores the correction closes about twenty feet out and the corners do not meet.

Which makes this the least theoretical claim on the site. It is not a consequence of a model; it is a correction that survey crews were physically applying on the ground, for money, decades before anyone could photograph the Earth. The offset tables cited here are the 1910 edition; the grid itself dates to 1785, and standard parallels appear in the manuals at least as far back as 1855, though the 1855 text treats them as a fix for meridian convergency and does not yet set out the tangent-and-secant procedure. The offsets in the table above are computed here from the geodesic curvature tan φ ÷ R rather than read out of the historical tables; they agree with the published one-mile entry, and anyone wanting the surveyed figures can check Table 13 directly.

And your instinct is right about everything a pilot or driver would experience. At 45°N and 560 mph the bank angle needed to hold the parallel is 3.4 arcminutes. You would feel nothing and see nothing. Nobody commands that turn either — you command a heading, and the turn is simply what holding that heading means on a sphere. The autopilot supplies it without any human deciding to.

So both intuitions are correct about different things. “I am not turning” is right about the controls and the compass. “You are turning” is right about the path. The arbiter is a gyro, because it holds its orientation against space rather than against a reference that is quietly rotating underneath it — which is the same instrument, and the same reason, as §4.

Worth saying plainly, because it is a point in the chapter's favour. This distinction defeats most people who think the Earth is round, and the usual reply to this chapter — “the equator is a great circle, so no turn is needed” — is true but answers a question nobody asked. It does not explain why the pilot's experience feels the same at every latitude. A challenge that exposes a gap that wide in the standard defence is not a cheap one.

Finally, if the whole framing still feels like a definitional trick, close the circuit and it stops being one. Go all the way round a parallel and come back facing the way you started. Something rotated through a full turn. The only question is how much of it you supplied and how much the surface did:

At the pole you are circling a point, you supply all of it, and the two models agree exactly. At the equator the planet supplies all of it and you supply none, and they disagree by a full revolution. No map, no scale, no distances, and nothing to argue about.

And nobody actually flies a parallel anyway

Holding a line of latitude is a real manoeuvre with a real turn rate, and that is what the table computes. It is also something no airline has ever asked a crew to do, and that matters for how the other rows should be read.

On a globe the shortest path between two points is a great circle, and a great circle is not a line of latitude. It bows toward the pole, sometimes enormously. Which is why holding a constant heading — the intuitive way to “go straight” — is the expensive way to travel:

London and Tokyo both sit in the low fifties and thirties, and the aeroplane between them goes to 71°N. That is not a detour; it is the short way, by a thousand miles, and it is flown daily with the fuel bill to prove it. The middle column is the rhumb line — the route you fly by holding one heading — rather than “along the parallel,” which is not defined for two cities at different latitudes.

Which is the real reason the equator is the right route, and it is a better reason than the one in §1. The equator is the only line of latitude that is also a great circle. Everywhere else on Earth, “hold this parallel” and “don't steer” are two different instructions and an aircraft can only obey one of them. At the equator they are the same instruction. That is what makes the comparison fair: it is the one latitude where both models can be asked about the same manoeuvre, and where a pilot doing nothing at all is a valid run of the experiment.

And it is worth saying what the northern rows really show, because it is a point in the chapter's favour. Compare the two models on the route aircraft do fly — the shortest one — and they nearly agree. On the flat map the shortest path between London and Tokyo also bows poleward, peaking near 74.6°N against the globe's 70.9°N. A few degrees of latitude, a couple of hundred miles of cross-track: real, measurable, but not the sort of thing that would jump out of a cabin window. The northern hemisphere has never been where this map gets into trouble, and the chapter is right not to look for the argument there.

The trouble is south, where the same projection stretches the 45th parallel by 233% and the 60th by 424% — which is why p. 120 spends its effort questioning whether southern distances are real rather than defending what the map does with them.

4 · But not on the instruments the chapter has in mind

Here is where the challenge gets more interesting than either side usually allows, and where a lazy rebuttal would go wrong. 5.15°/hr sounds large because it is quoted per hour. Spread across a flight it is not something a pilot would ever feel or see on the panel.

  • — The bank angle is 2.2 arcminutes. A coordinated turn at 5.15°/hr and 560 mph needs the aircraft banked by four hundredths of one degree. No attitude indicator in any cockpit resolves that, and the p. 119 discussion of pendulous vanes is correct that it would not help if one did.
  • — The turn needle would not move. A standard-rate turn is 3° per second. This is one two-thousandth of that.
  • — And the compass would read the same in both models. This is the subtle one. On the flat map, “north” at any point means toward the centre. Fly the equator circle and the pole stays exactly abeam the whole way, so the heading indicator sits on 090° — the same reading a globe would give. (True heading, throughout: magnetic declination shifts by a few degrees along this route, from about 4°W at Quito to near zero at Pontianak, and a pilot chasing it would be making real heading changes — but that drift is a property of the Earth's magnetic field, identical under either model, and says nothing about shape.) The reference turns with you. A magnetic heading alone cannot separate the models, and any rebuttal claiming it can is wrong.

So the chapter is right that this is not visible in the way a pilot might first imagine. What it misses is that the aeroplane is already carrying an instrument that does not share the compass's problem, and has been carrying one since the first 747s went into service around 1970.

A gyroscope does not know where the pole is. It holds its orientation against inertial space, which is why a directional gyro drifts against the compass and why pilots reset it. That drift is not a defect; it is a measurement. Its rate is Earth rate, 15.04°/hr times the sine of the latitude, plus a transport term for the aircraft's own motion. At the equator, flying east, both terms are exactly zero — sin 0° = 0 and tan 0° = 0. On the flat map, the transport term is 5.15°/hr and does not vanish. The globe says the gyro and the compass stay locked together for the entire fourteen-hour flight. The flat map says they separate by 76°.

The sensor resolves that with room to spare: a navigation-grade ring-laser gyro holds bias to roughly 0.01°/hr, and the signal in dispute is some five hundred times larger. But the chapter's pitch is “pull the flight recorder,” and that is where this answer needs a correction against itself.

The certified black box will not show it, and we should say so. Under 14 CFR Part 121 Appendix M, roll attitude is a mandated parameter at 0.5° resolution and ±2° accuracy, sampled once a second. The bank in question is 0.04° — twelve times below the recording resolution and fifty times below the accuracy specification. Raw gyro body rates are not mandated parameters at all. And the recorded heading is fed from the inertial reference after earth-rate and transport corrections have been applied inside the box, so it reads 090° under either model. Pull the FDR off an equator flight and the divergence is not in the file.

So the honest version of the answer is narrower than “check the black box,” and it is worth naming what would actually work:

  • — Raw inertial-reference output, or a quick-access recorder configured to log body rates. That exists, but it needs the operator's cooperation, which a chartered jet may or may not give.
  • — Or bring your own. A gyro in a seat, logging to a laptop, next to a GPS. Navigation-grade (0.01°/hr) is tens of thousands to buy but can be rented; tactical-grade, at 0.5–1°/hr of bias stability, costs a few thousand and will resolve 5.15°/hr provided its bias is calibrated stationary before and after and the leg is long enough to average. Nobody's permission required, and it rides any scheduled sector that crosses the equator.
And one thing to be clear about before anyone tries it, because it is the obvious way to get a wrong answer. A gyro in a seat does not read zero on a globe. It reads the earth's rotation, plus the transport term from the aircraft's own motion, plus every genuine heading change at every waypoint. The comparison that means something is integrated gyro yaw against heading change derived from the GPS track. The globe predicts the difference between them to be the integral of (15.04 sin φ + V tan φ ÷ R), which is zero only on the equator — which is the whole reason for flying that line. The flat map predicts the integral of V÷r instead, and that is never zero anywhere.

5 · The island of pilots — and why the two directions are not alike

The chapter closes with a good rhetorical move. Imagine pilots who have never seen a globe. Introduce the model and they say: “But we never point our nose down to go around the curve, and there is no adjustment for Earth spin. That's impossible.”

It is a fair thing to ask. The rate at which the nose would have to drop is V÷R, which at 560 mph is 8.1° per hour — about a seventh of a degree per minute, far below anything a person would notice performing. But that is not really the answer, because a pilot does not perform it at all.

The mechanism is the one he described correctly himself, two pages earlier. The pendulous vanes erect the attitude indicator to local vertical — whatever is locally down, continuously. On a globe, local vertical rotates as you travel, at exactly V÷R. So an aircraft flown level against that indicator is following the curve automatically, with nobody pointing anything anywhere. The chapter's own analysis of the instrument, which is right, is precisely why the island pilots never feel the thing they are being asked to feel.

The objection has the two directions the wrong way round

And this is where the argument turns over, because “you would have to constantly adjust” assumes that following a curve takes work. In the vertical it does not, and in the horizontal it does — which is the opposite of what the objection needs.

  • — Vertically, the aeroplane is held on the curve by physics. It flies at a constant pressure altitude, and an isobaric surface wraps the planet. Lift balances weight along local vertical. Try to fly a Euclidean straight line instead and you would be climbing away from that surface, losing airspeed and lift until you settled back onto it. It is an equilibrium with a restoring force, and it costs nothing to maintain. Level flight is curved flight.
  • — Horizontally there is no aerodynamic mechanism at all. Air does not push an aeroplane sideways, and there is no trim state or restoring force that puts it on one particular curve. Whatever horizontal curvature exists has to come from bank, or — away from the equator — from Coriolis.
Two honest limits on that, because a pilot will raise both. First, aircraft carry small persistent roll tendencies from rigging, fuel imbalance and aileron trim, so a one-directional 0.04° bank held for fourteen hours is indistinguishable from a slightly out-of-trim aeroplane. Second, a genuinely hands-off transport does not hold wings level indefinitely; its spiral mode is neutral or mildly divergent, and away from the equator Coriolis curves the track anyway. So the discriminator cannot be “did the pilot act.” It has to be the two things that survive both objections: whether a gyro referenced to inertial space disagrees with a heading referenced to the pole (§4), and whether the aircraft leaves the line (below).

So the flat model needs the kind of curve that cannot happen by itself, sustained for fourteen hours, while the globe needs the kind that cannot be avoided. That asymmetry is not a debating point — it is what makes the experiment worth flying, and it suggests a cleaner version of it.

The sharpest form of the test: take your hands off

Fly the equator eastbound, then take the steering out of the loop while leaving the aeroplane flyable. Concretely: roll-attitude hold at zero bank — an autopilot wings-level mode, or a pilot flying zero bank against the attitude indicator — with no heading reference and no lateral navigation engaged. That is what “hands off” has to mean here, and it is deliberately not “let go of everything”: as noted above, a transport left entirely alone rolls off into its spiral mode and the test becomes a test of the autopilot's absence. Hold the wings level, steer nothing, and both models make a prediction that the other cannot match.

On a globe, you stay on the equator. Two separate things that would normally pull you off it both vanish at exactly that latitude, and this is the second reason the route is well chosen. The geometric requirement, (V÷R) tan φ, is zero because tan 0° = 0. And the sideways Coriolis deflection, which is what forces the correction at every other latitude, goes as sin φ — also zero. At the equator, flying east, the Coriolis term points straight up, not sideways: it makes the aircraft very slightly lighter and does not steer it at all. The equator is the one line on Earth where an aeroplane left alone will simply follow it.

It is worth seeing how narrow that window is. Take the same hands-off aircraft to 45°N and its ground track curves right at 21.3°/hr from the Coriolis term alone — a hundred miles of drift in the first hour, and a full circle in under seventeen hours. The equator is the only place on the planet where an aeroplane can be left alone and still be doing something meaningful. Which is, once again, the route the chapter picked.

On the flat map, meanwhile, the equator is a circle around the pole and an aircraft left alone flies straight. So it leaves:

Away from the pole, so the drift is southward — into the southern hemisphere at roughly 25 miles in the first hour and accelerating, since the departure grows as the square of the distance flown. Worth pre-empting one misreading of the chapter's “opposite adjustments on the return trip”: flown straight, the return leg drifts south as well, because the flat equator curves away from a tangent in the same direction whichever way you travel along it. It is the required bank that reverses sign, not the departure.

One confound has to be handled, and it is a serious one. The aeroplane flies through moving air. A crosswind of just 22 knots puts you 25 miles off track in an hour — which is the flat map's entire first-hour prediction, produced by ordinary weather. Winds of that size at cruise level are unremarkable, even near the equator. So the raw position at the end of the leg proves nothing on its own, and any version of this page that says otherwise can be dismissed by a pilot in one sentence.

Two things separate wind from geometry, and both come out of the same log:

  • — Shape. Wind drift accumulates linearly with time — twice the time, twice the offset. The flat map's departure grows as the square of distance flown: 25 miles in the first hour, 100 in two, 391 in four. Two hours of logging separates a straight line from a parabola.
  • — What moves. Wind changes your track relative to your heading — that difference is drift angle, and the FMS displays it along with the wind vector it is already computing. Geometry does something else entirely: on the flat map a straight-flying aircraft's pole-referenced heading changes at 5.15°/hr while its yaw rate stays at zero. Heading against track, from the same instruments, tells you which one you are looking at.
With the wind accounted for, this is still the version needing no arcminutes. A hundred miles off track after two hours is a GPS readout, a radar fix, a coastline out of the window, a noon sun-line. The signal is four orders of magnitude larger than the 0.04° bank in §4 and it lands as a position, which is the one quantity nobody has to take on trust. And it tests what his version tests with the pilot — the disputed part of the loop — taken out.

The second half of the island-pilots objection, “no adjustment for Earth spin,” has the same shape of answer and is worked through on the rotation page: the adjustment exists, it is the Coriolis correction, and inertial navigation systems apply it continuously at every latitude except the one this flight happens to follow. The pilot does not apply it because the box does.

6 · Every flight is the experiment, not just this one

The chapter budgets $715,000 and hopes to interest a sponsor. It does not need one, and this is the part worth landing properly, because it is stronger than “airliners fly and they seem fine.” The measurement does not depend on the route. Any two airports, any heading, any latitude. The equator flight is a clean single-variable version of something the whole fleet is already doing.

The reason is that a flight recorder does not merely log where the aeroplane ended up. It logs a closed loop, and a closed loop is a much harder thing to fake than a destination. Three quantities go into it:

  • 1. What the aircraft was told to do — control inputs, autopilot commands, selected heading and track.
  • 2. What it actually did — every rotation and acceleration, measured by gyros and accelerometers against inertial space. Not against a map, not against the ground, not against any assumption about the world outside. A gyro has no opinion about the shape of the Earth.
  • 3. Where it ended up — position, cross-checked continuously against GPS and ground stations.
Those three have to reconcile, and the thing that reconciles them is a model of the Earth's shape. Commands plus measured rotations must predict the resulting track. To get from “the gyros turned through this much” to “so we are now here,” the navigation computer needs the planet's radius, its rotation rate and its gravity field, and they sit in the mechanisation as explicit constants. If the shape were wrong, the loop would not close — the aeroplane would arrive somewhere other than where its own instruments said it was going, on every flight, in a direction that depended on latitude.

Which is the cleanest way to say what this chapter is really asking for. The log is an equation, and the shape of the Earth is one of the terms in it. Any flight supplies enough of the other terms to solve for it, which is exactly why the route does not matter — Quito to Pontianak, Heathrow to Newark, a positioning leg round the pattern. They all close, or none of them do.

What the equator buys, then, is not validity. It is elegance. Fly it and three separate terms go to zero at once — the globe's required turn, the track correction, and the lateral Coriolis deflection — so the equation stops comparing two numbers and starts comparing a number with nothing. Everywhere else on Earth the two models predict quantities that merely differ. At the equator one of them predicts zero, and a measurement against zero is the sharpest kind there is. His instinct about the route was right; it was only the arithmetic that needed fixing.

And the terms in that mechanisation are the ones §3 has already computed:

Which answers the island pilots directly, and better than §5 did. “There is no adjustment for Earth spin” — there is, it is the first row, and it is applied several hundred times a second on every aircraft carrying an inertial reference. The pilots never see it because it happens inside a box the size of a shoebox, not because it is absent.

And the loop is checked to about a mile an hour. A navigation-grade inertial system dead-reckons with roughly a nautical mile per hour of drift — some fifteen nautical miles across a fourteen-hour flight — and the crew watches that residual against GPS the whole way. Now put the wrong planet in it. Stretch the radius by the π/2 a flat map would need and the transport terms come out 36% wrong. The resulting position error is worked out below, and it runs to hundreds of miles in the first hour — not a subtle degradation anyone could overlook, but the navigator openly disagreeing with the window.

The box measures the size of the Earth, with no windows

There is a sharper version still, and it is the one to leave a reader with. An inertial platform has to be Schuler tuned: its error loop is set so the instrument behaves like a pendulum whose length is the radius of the Earth. That gives it a characteristic period,

T = 2π √(R ÷ g) = 84.4 minutes

and it is the period at which every inertial navigator's error is observed to oscillate. Run it backwards — solve for R from the 84.4 minutes — and out comes 6,370 kilometres.

A sealed instrument, bolted in a bay, with no view of the sky, no map, and no reference to anything outside the aircraft, returns the radius of the Earth to three significant figures. And it is not read off a dial — it is the value that has to be in the loop for the platform to hold vertical at all.

It is worth doing that properly rather than asserting it. Mechanise the loop with the wrong radius and the platform does not become unstable; it acquires a tilt that grows with distance flown, because the rate at which the navigator commands the platform to rotate no longer matches the rate at which local vertical actually rotates. The mismatch is V × (1/Rtrue − 1/Rassumed). Put the flat map's 6,225 miles in place of 3,963 and after a single hour at 560 mph the platform is tilted by

560 × (1/3,963 − 1/6,225) = 0.051 rad = 2.9° off vertical

and twice that after two hours — which means the accelerometers are reading about 0.05 g of horizontal acceleration that is not there, and rising, and integrating it twice into position. That is the mechanism behind the hundreds-of-miles-per-hour failure, stated as a mechanism rather than a percentage.

And the alignment before pushback is a second, independent check

Before it navigates anything, an inertial reference unit sits still and senses the Earth's rotation vector — magnitude 15.04°/hr — resolving it into a horizontal and a vertical component. The horizontal part points at true north, which is how the aircraft finds north with no magnetic sensor at all. The ratio of the two components gives the latitude directly, which the unit checks against the position the crew typed in. A gross mismatch and the alignment fails and the aircraft does not go.

That procedure assumes the rotation vector is fixed in space and that its angle to local vertical is the co-latitude — and it is tested at every airport, at every latitude, tens of thousands of times a day. It also degrades exactly where the geometry says it should: north-finding depends on the horizontal component, which goes as cos φ, so alignments take progressively longer toward the poles and eventually will not complete. The same effect makes gyrotheodolites unusable within roughly 15° of the pole, which is covered on the rotation page — and which has no meaning at all on a flat plane.

So the honest reply to the challenge is not that nobody has run the experiment. It is that the experiment runs continuously, on every commercial flight in the world, as a condition of the aircraft being allowed to depart — and that the industry doing it would notice within a single sector if the answer changed.

7 · The follow-up experiment, which is the better one

The chapter does not stop at the equator. It proposes a second flight, and this one goes almost unremarked: “start from the bottom of India, Africa, or Australia and fly a route that perfectly follows magnetic South.”

That is a sharper test than the equator run, and it is worth saying so. Southbound tracks are where the two models part company completely rather than by percentages, because they disagree not about a rate but about a topology:

  • — On a globe, southbound tracks converge. Depart south from Cape Town, Perth and Punta Arenas and the three routes get closer together the further you go, meet at a single point, and cross to the other side. The far ends of those flights are near each other.
  • — On any pole-centred flat map, they diverge. Southbound means radially outward, so the three routes spread apart without limit and never meet. There is no point they share, and the far ends are further apart than the near ends.

Those are not adjustable by a scale factor; one has the routes meeting and the other has them separating.

But the obvious evidence is the wrong evidence, and it is worth saying why. The famous Antarctic flights — Hi Fly's A340 into Wolf's Fang, Norse's 787 into Troll, the Australian sightseeing charters — run almost straight south. That is precisely the direction in which the flat map is designed to be right: an azimuthal-equidistant chart preserves distance along its radii by construction, so a leg flown due south down a single meridian comes out identical on both. Cape Town to Troll is 16° off a meridian and so it does differ — 2,700 miles against 3,800, a factor of 1.4 — but that is a weak test dressed up as a strong one, and it is not something a passenger on one flight can see at all.

What discriminates is any leg with a substantial east–west component down there, because that is where the map's circumference has to stretch. Two exist and are flown:

LegOn a globeOn the flat mapRatio
Cape Town → Troll (nearly radial)2,700 mi3,800 mi1.4×

The last of those is the one to keep. Novolazarevskaya sits at 11.8°E and Progress at 76.4°E, both around 70°S — a straightforward east–west hop between two research stations, flown by Basler BT-67 in a working day as part of the DROMLAN feeder network. On the globe it is 1,450 miles. On a pole-centred flat map those two stations are on a circle 44,000 miles around, and the hop becomes 11,800 miles — a fortnight's flying for a piston-turbine aircraft with a range under 2,000. It is flown routinely, so the map is wrong by a factor of eight.

And the transpolar version has been flown commercially, once, with passengers and a published schedule. Pan Am Flight 50, 28–30 October 1977, a 747SP named Clipper New Horizons: San Francisco over the North Pole to London, London to Cape Town, then Cape Town to Auckland across the South Pole, and home — the whole circuit in 54 hours 7 minutes. That third leg reaches 74°S and runs 7,300 miles on a globe. On the flat map it is 17,000, which is beyond the range of any aircraft ever built.

One clarification on the chapter's own wording. It asks for a route following magnetic south, which leads to the south magnetic pole at roughly 64°S 136°E, off Adélie Land — a real place in either model, so the compass heading is not itself the discriminator. What discriminates is the shape of the tracks: whether routes running south from different continents draw together or spread apart.

So the chapter's own second experiment answers its first. The equator run distinguishes the models by a number; the polar run distinguishes them by whether a destination exists at all. He proposed both. The second one has been flown, repeatedly, with passengers and a published schedule.

8 · What would settle it, and the cheap version

Two tests, in ascending order of cost.

  • 1. A gyro on a table. A navigation-grade fibre-optic or ring-laser gyro, stationary, measures Earth rate about the local vertical: 15.04°/hr × sin φ. Take it to two latitudes a few hundred miles apart and the reading changes by a predictable amount. No aircraft, no charter, no permits. This is the same measurement as the surveying test on the self-test page, and it settles the underlying question the flight is a proxy for.
  • 2. The wings-level leg, if the flight happens. Cheaper than the full route and stronger than it: hold zero bank over the equator with no heading or lateral-nav reference engaged, and log position and wind. The globe says you stay on the line; the flat map says 100 miles off it in two hours, growing as the square of the distance rather than linearly the way wind drift does. Record the FMS wind vector alongside, and the answer arrives as a position rather than an arcminute. Worth flying as a one-hour segment of any scheduled positioning sector.
  • 3. The full route, if somebody funds it. Worth doing — it would be a good piece of work and it would produce a public dataset. The prediction to record is the accumulated yaw of the inertial platform relative to the local horizontal frame, along the equator, eastbound. The globe says 0.0°, to within instrument bias. The flat map says 75.9° over 8,250 miles. Any sustained non-zero yaw rate at the equator, above the platform's bias, falsifies the globe on this measurement.

The honest bottom line

The chapter asks for raw data and numbers, and it is entitled to them. The numbers say the experiment was well chosen, that the equator is the right route for exactly the reason he intuited, and that his reading of the attitude indicator is correct. They also say the table's 119° was obtained with the globe's own circumference — and that the flat model, as the chapter deliberately leaves it, supplies no scale from which any rival figure could be computed.

None of which sinks the experiment, because the strongest form of it never needed the scale. One full circuit of the equator is 360° of turning on any flat surface and 0° on a globe, and the difference is the curvature itself. The aeroplane is not the instrument that settles it — the gyro is, and it settled it decades ago, on every route rather than this one; but a pilot who trims level over the equator and takes his hands off will get the answer out of the window inside two hours.

The chapter wanted raw numbers from a black box. There is a black box on every aircraft in the sky, it has been logging exactly those numbers since the first jets crossed an ocean without a navigator, and one of the numbers it quietly reports — from a sealed case with no view of anything — is that the Earth is 6,370 km in radius.

What remains is a genuinely good question with a settled answer, which is a better position for the chapter to be in than most of the book.
