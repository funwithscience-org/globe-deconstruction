Something Did Disappear Over a Flat Surface

The arithmetic on his slide is right. A globe hides nothing at 540 feet. So what hid the raft — and how would you find out?

On 18 August 2026, Shape Debate towed an inflatable lounger 540 feet across a stormwater pond and filmed it from a lens 0.375 inches above the water. The raft disappeared, low end first. This page grants the observation, checks every number on the calculation slide, measures what the footage actually contains — and then spends most of its length on the part that matters: the four changes that would turn a suggestive result into a decisive one. Three of them can be done at the same pond in an afternoon.

Observation concededMechanism unresolved
Where this actually stands

Objects really do disappear bottom-up over flat surfaces — this site said so before the video existed, about road mirages. His curvature arithmetic is correct to the digit, and no part of that raft was hidden by the shape of the Earth. But burying four inches of raft needs about 48× more hiding than curvature could supply at 540 feet, so something local and much stronger did the work. The footage narrows it: the raft dimmed rather than being cut off along a line, which rules out a hiding edge but not the three candidates that remain. One experiment separates them, and it is cheap.

1 · What the video gets right

Starting here is not politeness. Two of the claims are simply correct, and the rest of the page only makes sense once they are on the table.

The calculation slide is right, every line of it. Recomputed independently with R = 20,903,520 ft:

A globe hides nothing at that range, from any camera height he used, and the slide says so correctly. Anyone answering this video by disputing the geometry has not checked it.

One omission, and it runs in his favour

The slide uses pure geometric curvature — the true Earth radius, with no allowance for atmospheric refraction. Surveyors normally fold refraction in by inflating the radius, Reff = R ÷ (1 − k), with k around 0.13 to 0.17 — the old rule of thumb that you work with seven-sixths of the Earth's radius. It is worth doing, because anyone tempted to answer this video with “you forgot refraction” should first see which way it moves.

Standard refraction bends rays gently towards the Earth, which pushes the horizon further out and lowers the threshold height — so his lens ends up further above the threshold, not closer to it, and the ratio improves from 4.5× to about 5.2×. Hidden height stays exactly zero in every row. The omission costs him nothing, and correcting it strengthens his case rather than weakening it.

The one kind of refraction that does hide things here runs the other way: the hot-surface case, where the air immediately above the water is the least dense and rays bend upward instead of down. That is not curvature under another name. It is the local mechanism this page is about, and §2 puts the strength it would need at roughly 43× the Earth's own curvature — which is the whole difficulty in one number.

And the underlying claim is true. Objects can vanish bottom-up over a surface with no curvature at all. Every driver has seen it on a hot road, where the far end of the carriageway is replaced by a shimmering false sky and cars sink into it. Our own page on this conceded the point before the video was made. On the narrow existence claim he set himself, he is right, and he went and demonstrated it rather than arguing about it.

Worth noting too that the method visibly improves across the three attempts — fixing the camera and moving the target instead, thin string to avoid disturbing the water, correcting the raft's orientation mid-run so the long side stays square to the lens. Those are the instincts of someone actually trying to measure something.

2 · The 48× problem

Here is the difficulty, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion. Grant that the raft vanished. To bury a target standing 4 inches out of the water, something has to hide about four inches. Curvature at 540 feet can hide, at absolute maximum — measuring from a lens at zero height — 0.0837 inches.

Whatever hid that raft was roughly fifty times stronger than curvature could be at that range. For curvature alone to bury four inches, the raft would have to sit 4,876 feet away from his actual eye height — nine times further down the pond than it was. He has not found a small curvature effect. He has found a large local one.

That number cuts in an unexpected direction. It is not an objection to his experiment — it is the reason his experiment cannot be scaled up to the ship-and-horizon case it is aimed at. A mechanism strong enough to hide four inches at 540 feet is enormously stronger than curvature at that distance, so its presence at a pond says nothing about what happens at eight miles, where the curvature term has grown by a factor of 6,400 and the surface-hugging term has not.

Which makes it an edge case — and that is the useful finding

An edge case is not a worthless result. It is a real, repeatable effect produced by a configuration chosen precisely because it produces the effect, and knowing where a mechanism switches on is worth having. The difficulty is only with what gets read off it afterwards.

The clearest way to see this is to set the pond beside the observation it is meant to explain — because they are opposite anomalies, produced by the same thin layer of air.

On the left is the classic case: a sightline held within inches of a water surface, over which distant objects stay stubbornly visible when curvature says they should be well buried. That is the Bedford Level result of 1838, and it is the argument of the Rampion wind-farm footage, which we answer on our own Rampion page — turbine bases apparently meeting the sea at 8 to 11 miles, where curvature from a beach-level camera should hide roughly 23 and 53 feet. The conclusion drawn is that there is no curvature.

On the right is this pond: a sightline held within inches of a water surface, over which a nearby object vanishes completely when curvature says nothing whatever should be hidden. The conclusion drawn is that hiding does not require curvature.

One rule covers both, and it is worth stating plainly: light bends towards denser air. When the water is cooler than the air above it, the densest air lies at the bottom, rays curve gently downward with the surface, and distant objects stay visible past the point where geometry says they should have gone — looming, and the superior mirage. When the water is warmer, the gradient reverses, rays curve upward instead, and objects vanish early. One mechanism, one layer, two signs, decided by which way the temperature runs. That single account covers the Bedford Level, Rampion and this pond, which is why we are content to hold all three at once.

What cannot be held at once is both of them as evidence against a globe. The first argument only works if the near-surface air is optically quiet — because if it is bending rays downward, then an object staying visible is telling you about the air, not about the curvature. The second argument needs that same air to be strong enough to bury four inches at 540 feet. One requires the layer to be doing nothing; the other requires it to be doing a great deal. And once it is doing anything at all, the visibility of a low object stops being a measurement of the planet's shape.

Which is the honest reading of the pond result. It establishes that the near-surface layer is real, that it can be strong, and that it can be demonstrated on demand. That is a genuine contribution — and it argues against using low-level observations as curvature evidence, in either direction.

3 · What the footage actually measures

We retrieved the source files from his own Drive folder — three setup clips and the uncropped eleven-minute run — and measured rather than watched. Four things came out.

The raft dimmed. It was not cut off.

Tracking the raft's peak brightness against the background immediately beside it, through the fade:

The background never moves. It sits between 109 and 118 for the whole sequence, with no trend. The raft's own brightness falls smoothly until the contrast hits the noise floor and stays there. Nothing rises up through the raft; the raft fades out where it stands.

That shape rules out a hiding line, whatever caused it. Occlusion from below — by water, by a crest, by a refracted horizon — removes the bottom of an object while the top stays at full brightness, and it puts something brighter in front of what it removes. Neither happened. This is the signature of a target fading below a detection threshold, which is a different thing from a target being hidden. A YouTube commenter got there independently, describing it as disappearing from the inside out.

Three candidates remain, and all three were present

  • — Ripples, with a brutal lever arm. Occlusion by a near wave crest scales as the ratio of target distance to crest distance. A crest of half an inch fifteen feet from the lens hides 4.9 inches at 540 feet — the whole raft. At fifteen feet the amplification is 36×; at the midpoint of the pond, where his calm-water check was filmed, it is 2×. The check was taken at the one place along the sightline where surface state barely matters.
  • — Near-field vegetation, and this one is not an inference. In the opening seconds of the uncropped run the raft is visible through near-shore reeds — the white hull and its orange stripe broken up by grass stems inches from the lens. The occluders are in the light path on camera.
  • — A thermal gradient, and the site favours it. Hiding four inches at this range needs a lapse rate of about −7.9 °C/m near the surface — 0.78 °C across the lowest ten centimetres. That sounds like a lot until you remember what it is over: a shallow, dark-bottomed suburban retention pond at 11 a.m. in August, holding the previous day's heat under air that cooled overnight. Warm water under cooler air is precisely the superadiabatic case that bends rays downward and hides things. Nobody measured it, so this stays a hypothesis — but it is the one a physicist would bet on, and two thermometers would settle it.

The focus collapsed exactly where the raft went — and his answer to that is fair

Measuring the treeline's edge sharpness every ten seconds across the whole run, the camera holds a steady 8 pixels for the first six minutes, degrades to 20–22 pixels between t = 400 and 470 s, then recovers to 8 px by the end. The raft fades between t ≈ 415 and 450 s — inside the worst-focused stretch of the eleven minutes, with the camera 2.5× softer than at either end.

He anticipates this, in a caption: the trees come back into focus and the raft is still not there. That is true, and it is a fair point. What it establishes is that the raft stayed invisible once sharpness returned. What it cannot establish is why it went in the first place, because by then the raft had been at maximum range for three and a half minutes and was at the edge of detectability regardless.

4 · What the footage cannot say

One limit is set by the camera rather than the pond, and it decides how much the footage could ever have shown. Matching the skyline between the uncropped run and the published frames puts the framing at the phone's telephoto end. At that framing the raft stands about 4 pixels tall, against a measured edge response — the smallest transition the delivered footage can render — of 9 pixels.

The raft's entire height is about half of the smallest edge this camera can draw.

A profile that short cannot display bottom-up truncation, because there is no room within it for a line to move up through. Worth being precise about what that does and does not mean: it does not make the disappearance unreal, and it does not mean the raft was invisible. It means the shape of the disappearance — cut off from below, versus faded out where it stood — was never something these frames could resolve on the raft itself. That is why §3 measures brightness instead, which the frames can resolve, and why §5c recommends a target tall enough that the question becomes readable.

Three further limits, stated plainly:

  • — We cannot say which mechanism dominated. Ripples, refraction and defocus were all present, all act in the same direction, and this footage cannot separate them. Neither can we; neither can he.
  • — The verification clips were filmed after the run, not during it. Camera metadata puts the attempt at 11:07–11:19 and the “no obstruction” and “water is calm” checks at 11:32–11:40 — thirteen to twenty-one minutes later. On a sunny August morning, wind and the near-surface thermal profile both move on that timescale. The checks document the pond; they are not measurements of the conditions during the disappearance. This is an easy fix, not a fault.
  • — The distance-exponent test cannot be rescued from this run. We tried. Range recovery from the raft's apparent length fails once it is broken up by reeds and then unresolved, hidden height is not measurable at four pixels against a nine-to-twenty-two pixel blur, and the point-spread function moves 2.5× mid-run. It is the right test; it has to be designed in beforehand.

5 · How to make this decisive

This is the part worth the reader's attention, and it is offered in the spirit the experiment was run in. The pond test as it stands establishes that something hid the raft. Four changes would establish what, and three of them are an afternoon's work at the same pond.

None of what follows is new, and that is the point. The Bedford Level experiment was run the same way in 1838 — telescope eight inches above the water — and settled in 1870 when Alfred Russel Wallace redid it with two changes: he raised the sightline to thirteen feet, out of the near-surface layer, and he added a marker at the midpoint so the geometry could be read rather than argued. Both of those are below, as §5a and §5c. The full account is in §6 of Bottom-Up Observations. We are not proposing a novel dodge; we are proposing the correction that resolved this exact experimental design a century and a half ago.

5a · Raise everything six feet, and change nothing else

Put the camera on a tripod at six feet and the target on a stand at six feet, same 540-foot separation, same pond, same day. The point is that the candidate mechanisms disagree sharply about what should happen, and one of them disagrees with all the others.

MechanismWhat it predicts when both ends rise six feet
Earth's curvatureNo change. Horizon moves to about three miles; 540 ft is still nothing. Hidden height stays zero.
Angular resolution / perspectiveNo change. Angular size is unaltered, and the slant range changes by about 6 mm in 540 ft — one part in 27,000.
Near-surface refractionEffect largely disappears. The strong gradient lives in the lowest metre or so over water; at six feet the ray is above it for its whole path.
Ripple occlusionEffect disappears. Crests are a surface phenomenon and the lever arm collapses once the sightline clears them.
Near-field vegetationEffect disappears. The reeds are at water level.
That is a clean two-way split, and it is the one that matters. If the raft still vanishes from six feet, every surface-hugging explanation is dead and the angular-resolution case becomes far stronger than this video currently makes it. If it stays visible, the effect is confined to the first few inches above the water — which is exactly the regime that does not scale to a ship at eight miles. Both outcomes are publishable, and neither depends on trusting anybody's arithmetic.

One honest caveat on our own prediction: the near-surface gradient does not switch off at some height, it weakens. Expect the effect to drop by a large factor at six feet, not to vanish to precisely zero. If the raft becomes markedly harder to lose but can still be coaxed away, that is informative too — and it is a result no amount of arguing produces.

5b · Then put a board in the way, deliberately

The proposal that goes with the six-foot test is a level board set in front of the target as an artificial horizon — and it is a good idea, though we would frame what it does slightly differently. It is not really a test of the mechanism. It is a calibrated hiding line at a known height, and what it answers is the question this whole dispute keeps running into:

What does four inches of genuine, deliberate occlusion actually look like through this camera, at this range?

Nobody knows, because it has never been filmed. Set the board to cut off a known amount of the target — two inches, say — and shoot it at the same framing. Two things come out at once. First, whether bottom-up truncation is resolvable at all at four pixels of target height: if a real board doing real hiding cannot produce a visible cut line, then no pond footage at that framing could ever have shown one. Second, the morphology of true occlusion, to hold against the fade: a hiding edge should give a sharp cut with the surviving top at full brightness, which is emphatically not what the photometry above shows.

5c · Swap the raft for something you can read

This is the single biggest improvement available, and it costs a plank of wood. The reason the run cannot be analysed further is that the target is four inches tall and the camera's smallest resolvable transition is about nine pixels against a raft height of four — so how much was hidden was never a measurable quantity. It was only ever visible or not visible.

Replace the lounger with a vertical staff, three feet tall, painted in two-inch bands, alternating high-contrast. Now the observable is a number: the lowest band still visible. Hidden height can be read directly, at every distance, on every frame, without fitting anything. This is not a novel idea — it is what Wallace used on the Bedford Level in 1870, and it is why that experiment produced a result while Rowbotham's produced an argument.

With a readable staff, the decisive measurement becomes available: how hidden height grows with distance. The three mechanisms separate cleanly, because each has its own exponent in H = e + A·dn:

MechanismExponent n
A near obstruction (crest, reeds)≈ 0.9 — close to linear
A uniform refracting layer≈ 1.8
Earth's curvature≈ 2.6 — and not reachable inside 4,876 ft

Walk the staff out in marked stages, read the lowest visible band at each, plot log H against log d. The slope names the mechanism. That is a real experiment with a real discriminator, which is the same compliment we paid the equator-flight challenge and meant just as literally.

5d · Two cameras, one instant — and lock them down

Two smaller changes that remove most of what is currently unarguable.

  • — Film both heights simultaneously. One phone at 0.375 in, one at six feet, same target, same second, ideally on the same tripod mast. This is what makes the comparison in §5a airtight: filming the two heights twenty minutes apart leaves wind, surface state and the thermal profile free to change in between, which is the same objection that applies to the verification clips.
  • — Lock focus, exposure and zoom, on a tripod. The autofocus excursion documented above is the single most damaging technical problem with the current run, and on an iPhone it is a long press to defeat. Also state the zoom used — the whole imaging argument on this page had to be reconstructed by matching skylines because the framing was never given.
  • — Two thermometers and a wind reading. One at 1 cm above the water, one at 50 cm, plus water temperature. Under a pound. If the gradient is there, it is the answer; if it is not, refraction is out and the remaining candidates get much easier to separate. Right now the most likely explanation on the table is also the only one nobody measured.
And one free improvement suggested by a commenter, which is better than anything above: run it again in winter, on a frozen lake. Ice removes the ripples and the near-shore vegetation outright, and water near freezing removes the superadiabatic case. If the disappearance survives that, the local explanations are in real trouble — and it would be the strongest version of this experiment anyone has filmed.

6 · What would change our mind

Stated in advance, because that is the standard this review has asked of the book and owes in return.

  • — A raft that still vanishes from six feet up, filmed simultaneously against a ground-level camera, with focus locked. That would kill ripples, reeds and near-surface refraction in one shot, and we would say so on this page.
  • — A hidden-height exponent near 2.6 from a graduated staff over a decent range of distances. We do not expect it inside 4,876 feet, and it would be a genuine surprise.
  • — A measured near-surface gradient of essentially zero during a run in which the target still disappears. That removes our own favoured explanation and we would have to go looking again.

What would not change our mind is a repeat of the same run at higher resolution. The problem with this experiment is not the camera; it is that four inches of target at 540 feet cannot distinguish between five mechanisms that all point the same way. Better pixels do not fix an underdetermined design. A readable staff and a second camera height do.

The last word should be the fair one. He built a rig, checked his own arithmetic, published the raw files, and invited criticism — and the arithmetic held up under it. Most of what is wrong with the run is wrong with every first attempt at a field measurement, and the fixes above are the ones any experimentalist would reach for second time round. We would rather see the second run than win the argument about the first.
