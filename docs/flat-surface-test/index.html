Something Did Disappear Over a Flat Surface

The arithmetic on his slide is right. A globe hides nothing at 540 feet. So what hid the raft — and how would you find out?

On 18 August 2026, Shape Debate towed an inflatable lounger 540 feet across a stormwater pond — Can an object disappear over a flat surface? first attempts — and filmed it from a lens 0.375 inches above the water. The raft disappeared, low end first. This page grants the observation, checks every number on the calculation slide, measures what the footage actually contains — and then spends most of its length on the part that matters: the five changes that would turn a suggestive result into a decisive one. Four of them can be done at the same pond in an afternoon.

Observation concededMechanism unresolved
Where this actually stands

Objects really do disappear bottom-up over flat surfaces — this site said so before the video existed, about road mirages. His curvature arithmetic is correct to the digit, and no part of that raft was hidden by the shape of the Earth. But burying four inches of raft needs about 48× more hiding than curvature could supply at 540 feet, so something local and much stronger did the work. Measuring the footage narrows it a long way. Refraction at the strength required is excluded by his own frames, and the leading explanation needs no weather at all: from a third of an inch up, the raft and the entire stretch of pond behind it are the same two arcminutes across — about four pixels, on a camera whose finest edge is nine. That is a fact about a very low camera, not about the Earth. Four cheap changes at the same pond would settle it.

1 · What the video gets right

Starting here is not politeness. Two of the claims are simply correct, and the rest of the page only makes sense once they are on the table.

One omission, and it runs in his favour

The slide uses pure geometric curvature — the true Earth radius, with no allowance for atmospheric refraction. Surveyors normally fold refraction in by inflating the radius, Reff = R ÷ (1 − k), with k around 0.13 to 0.17 — the old rule of thumb that you work with seven-sixths of the Earth's radius. It is worth doing, because anyone tempted to answer this video with “you forgot refraction” should first see which way it moves.

Standard refraction bends rays gently towards the Earth, which pushes the horizon further out and lowers the threshold height — so his lens ends up further above the threshold, not closer to it, and the ratio improves from 4.5× to about 5.2×. Hidden height stays exactly zero in every row. The omission costs him nothing, and correcting it strengthens his case rather than weakening it.

The one kind of refraction that does hide things here runs the other way: the hot-surface case, where the air immediately above the water is the least dense and rays bend upward instead of down. That is not curvature under another name. It is the local mechanism this page is about, and the strength it would need is the whole difficulty in one number: the light path would have to curve about eighty times more sharply than the Earth's surface does. The air over that pond would have to make it behave like a planet 49 miles in radius.

And the underlying claim is true. Objects can vanish bottom-up over a surface with no curvature at all. Every driver has seen it on a hot road, where the far end of the carriageway is replaced by a shimmering false sky and cars sink into it. Our own page on this conceded the point before the video was made. On the narrow existence claim he set himself, he is right, and he went and demonstrated it rather than arguing about it.

Worth noting too that the method visibly improves across the three attempts — fixing the camera and moving the target instead, thin string to avoid disturbing the water, correcting the raft's orientation mid-run so the long side stays square to the lens. Those are the instincts of someone actually trying to measure something.

2 · The 48× problem

Here is the difficulty, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion. Grant that the raft vanished. To bury a target standing 4 inches out of the water, something has to hide about four inches. Curvature at 540 feet can hide, at absolute maximum — measuring from a lens at zero height — 0.0837 inches.

Whatever hid that raft was roughly fifty times stronger than curvature could be at that range. For curvature alone to bury four inches, the raft would have to sit 4,876 feet away from his actual eye height — nine times further down the pond than it was. He has not found a small curvature effect. He has found a large local one.

That number cuts in an unexpected direction. It is not an objection to his experiment — it is the reason his experiment cannot be scaled up to the ship-and-horizon case it is aimed at. A mechanism strong enough to hide four inches at 540 feet is enormously stronger than curvature at that distance. From the same lens height, curvature hides 0.0837 inches at 540 feet and 40.4 feet at eight miles — it grows by a factor of about 5,800, because it goes as the square of the distance beyond the horizon. A fixed near obstruction grows only linearly, so curvature comes to dominate it. But we should not pretend the air simply drops out at range: near-surface refraction accumulates along the path, which is precisely why long, low sightlines at Bedford Level and Rampion end up dominated by the atmosphere rather than settled by it. The pond result does not transfer to eight miles — and neither does an assumption that the air is quiet there.

Which makes it an edge case — and that is the useful finding

An edge case is not a worthless result. It is a real, repeatable effect produced by a configuration chosen precisely because it produces the effect, and knowing where a mechanism switches on is worth having. The difficulty is only with what gets read off it afterwards.

The clearest way to see this is to set the pond beside the observation it is meant to explain — because they are opposite anomalies, produced by the same thin layer of air.

On the left is the classic case: a sightline held within inches of a water surface, over which distant objects stay stubbornly visible when curvature says they should be well buried. That is the Bedford Level result of 1838, and it is the argument of the Rampion wind-farm footage, which we answer on our own Rampion page — turbine bases apparently meeting the sea at 8 to 11 miles, where curvature from a beach-level camera should hide roughly 23 and 53 feet. The conclusion drawn is that there is no curvature.

On the right is this pond: a sightline held within inches of a water surface, over which a nearby object vanishes completely when curvature says nothing whatever should be hidden. The conclusion drawn is that hiding does not require curvature.

One rule covers both, and it is worth stating plainly: light bends towards denser air. When the water is cooler than the air above it, the densest air lies at the bottom, rays curve gently downward with the surface, and distant objects stay visible past the point where geometry says they should have gone — looming, and the superior mirage. When the water is warmer, the gradient reverses, rays curve upward instead, and objects vanish early. One mechanism, one layer, two signs, decided by which way the temperature runs. That single account covers the Bedford Level, Rampion and this pond, which is why we are content to hold all three at once.

What cannot be held at once is both of them as evidence against a globe. The first argument only works if the near-surface air is optically quiet — because if it is bending rays downward, then an object staying visible is telling you about the air, not about the curvature. The second argument needs that same air to be strong enough to bury four inches at 540 feet. One requires the layer to be doing nothing; the other requires it to be doing a great deal. And once it is doing anything at all, the visibility of a low object stops being a measurement of the planet's shape.

Which is the honest reading of the pond result. It establishes that the near-surface layer is real, that it can be strong, and that it can be demonstrated on demand. That is a genuine contribution — and it argues against using low-level observations as curvature evidence, in either direction.

3 · What the footage actually measures

We retrieved the source files from his own Drive folder — three setup clips and the uncropped eleven-minute run — and measured rather than watched. Four things came out.

The raft dimmed. It was not cut off.

The background never moves. It sits between 109 and 118 for the whole sequence, with no trend. The raft's own brightness falls smoothly until the contrast hits the noise floor and stays there. Nothing rises up through the raft; the raft fades out where it stands.

What this does and does not show. It is tempting to read a smooth fade as ruling out a hiding line, on the grounds that occlusion removes the bottom while the top stays bright. That reasoning holds for a resolved target, and this one is not resolved: four pixels of raft under a nine-pixel blur (§4). Cover the bottom 83% of an unresolved object and you remove 83% of its flux and therefore 83% of its peak, smoothly, with the cut line smeared away. So the fade is consistent with occlusion by a crest, by reeds, or by a mirage line sweeping up through it.

What does survive is narrower and still worth having: the background did not brighten. That is evidence against one specific candidate — a mirage vanishing line — because the false water it substitutes is sky luminance, and over a dark pond at eleven in the morning it should have arrived as a conspicuously brighter band, not an invisible one. It did not. Everything else the photometry establishes is that flux went away, which every remaining candidate predicts.

One piece of evidence narrows this, and it is already in his footage. The effect is height-dependent. The raft is plainly visible in the raised-camera shots and gone from a lens half an inch above the water, and that happened on all three attempts across the same morning. Two things follow immediately. Curvature is out, since it hides nothing at this range from any of those heights. And so is anything intermittent — a passing wave crest most of all — because the result repeated.

What height-dependence does not do is tell us which of the surviving candidates is responsible. The reason is worth seeing, because it turns out to explain most of the video on its own.

What a third of an inch actually does to the view

Put the lens that close to a flat surface and the geometry does something violent, entirely on its own, before any question of what the air is doing.

The raft is the same angular size as the whole pond behind it. Four inches at 540 feet subtends 2.12 arcminutes. Every foot of water from fifty feet out to the far bank subtends 2.15. From a third of an inch above the surface those are the same four pixels — the raft, the far water, the shoreline and the foot of the bank are all piled into one thin band at the horizon. The target is not being viewed against a background so much as buried in it.

This matters because it is a complete explanation on its own. A four-pixel object of modest contrast, sitting inside a four-pixel pile-up of everything else near the horizon, is a marginal detection in any weather. It goes when it goes, it stays gone, and the wedge shape of the lounger means the thin end goes first — all without the atmosphere doing anything at all.

It also disposes of the two facts that most look like evidence for refraction. Raising the camera to six feet does not only lift the ray clear of the near-surface layer; it decompresses the scene, opening that 2 arcminutes out to 374 and separating the raft from the horizon entirely. And the result repeats across three attempts because the geometry was the same on all three, not because a thermal layer persisted through the morning.

This is the leading explanation, and it rests on an arithmetic identity rather than a judgement about the weather. It was not what we expected to find — a near-surface thermal gradient looked the likelier candidate until the footage was checked for it (§3a). What we are confident of is narrower than a full account of the day: nothing here requires the atmosphere to be doing anything unusual, and nothing here bears on the shape of the Earth.

Two caveats on the height comparison, because the footage was not built for it: the raft was under tow, so the raised shots are not all at the same range as the low ones, and a raised camera sees the raft against a different background — which is itself part of the effect. Filming both heights at the same instant fixes both, which is why §5e asks for it.

Three candidates remain, and all three were present

  • — Ripples, with a brutal lever arm. Occlusion by a near wave crest scales as the ratio of target distance to crest distance. A crest of half an inch fifteen feet from the lens hides 4.9 inches at 540 feet — the whole raft. At fifteen feet the amplification is 36×; at the midpoint of the pond, where his calm-water check was filmed, it is 2×. The check was taken at the one place along the sightline where surface state barely matters.
  • — Near-field vegetation, and this one is not an inference. In the opening seconds of the uncropped run the raft is visible through near-shore reeds — the white hull and its orange stripe broken up by grass stems inches from the lens. The occluders are in the light path on camera.
  • — A thermal gradient — the candidate the footage rules out. The requirement is steep. Working from the ray that just grazes the water, rather than one launched level from the lens, hiding four inches at this range needs a near-surface lapse rate of about −14.7 °C per metre — 1.5 °C across the lowest ten centimetres. That is demanding but not absurd: over sun-heated asphalt far steeper gradients are ordinary, which is why road mirages are a daily event. It is also a path average, while real free-convection profiles fall away roughly as z−4/3, so the air in the first few centimetres is much steeper than the mean — and this sightline sits entirely in those centimetres. Over water the case is harder to make: water usually sits at or below air temperature, and evaporation, the cool skin and any breeze all work against a superadiabatic layer. This was the mechanism we expected to find. The footage says otherwise — §3a.

And a candidate that is not optics at all: the raft

Before reaching for the air, it is worth asking what the target was doing, because two properties of that particular object supply a good deal of the observation for free.

  • — It yaws, and the video says so. The method cards note that the raft's orientation is corrected mid-run so the long side stays square to the lens — which concedes that a 70-inch inflatable on two strings weathercocks in any breeze. Broadside it presents 37′ of length, some seventy pixels of streak; swung end-on it presents its width and perhaps half that. Peak brightness barely moves, but what makes something look present on a video is the streak, and detection improves roughly as the square root of the number of pixels you can integrate over. A yaw is a large, free contribution to “it's gone,” with no optics involved.
  • — It is a wedge, and a wedge fades bottom-first for nothing. The lounger stands four inches at the tall end and tapers to almost nothing at the other. The low end is thinner, carries less flux per column and sits closer to the water's own brightness — so under any uniform loss of contrast, whether haze, defocus, range or refraction, the low end crosses the detection threshold first. And if the raft yaws so the shallow end faces the camera, or if tow-line tension pulls the leading end down, “the low end went first” arrives with no mechanism whatever.
Which means the bottom-up ordering is the null expectation here, not a finding. This is the point we would most want made if the page were about someone else's argument, so it belongs in ours: until the target is rigid, weighted, non-yawing and of constant cross-section, “it disappeared from the bottom up” carries no information about why. It is one more reason the vertical staff of §5d is the right instrument — a graduated pole has the same profile from every angle and cannot present a thin end to the camera.

3a · The refraction test, run

A refracting layer strong enough to bury the raft does not hide quietly. It has an apparent horizon of its own, and that horizon is close.

Curving light eighty times more sharply than the Earth's surface puts the mirage's own vanishing line about 127 feet from the lens — forty metres out on a five-hundred-and-forty-foot sightline. The pond's surface should visibly end a short way in front of the camera and be replaced by a band of false sky; the far treeline should be truncated at its base and carry an inverted image beneath it; anything floating in between should sit above its own mirrored copy. This is the ordinary appearance of a hot road, and it is unmistakable.

None of it is there. Checked across all eleven minutes of the uncropped run, including every frame of the fade: the water reads as one continuous surface to the far bank, the green bank stays visible below the treeline throughout, and there is no false-water band and no inversion at any point. Better than a bare absence, there is a positive counter-indicator — through the calm stretches erect reflections run unbroken down to the waterline, which is the signature of a normal or inverted profile, not the strongly superadiabatic one this would require. Refraction at the strength needed to hide four inches is excluded by the footage.

Two bounds on that. It excludes the strength, not the phenomenon: a weak layer contributing a fraction of an inch is entirely possible and is not ruled out this way. And a null is only as good as what it looked for — a visible band would have been decisive on its own, whereas this is an absence sustained across eleven minutes with a counter-indicator beside it. Strong, not conclusive.

This is not the answer we expected. Of the candidates available, a near-surface gradient was the one most likely to carry an effect this large, and the arithmetic says it would have been sufficient. It simply was not present.

One smaller check rode along on the same frames: atmospheric haze is out by an order of magnitude. Contrast transmittance at this range costs about 6% in ten-kilometre visibility, against an observed loss of 83%.

What happened in attempt three, specifically

The third attempt — fixed camera, towed raft — contains an event the other two do not.

Around t = 380 s the surface changes. Until then it is glassy, with unbroken reflections running to the far shore. Ripple trains sweep in, the reflections break up, and the camera's focus starts to shift: between 400 and 470 s the treeline renders at 20–22 pixels against its usual 8. The raft's contrast collapses inside that window, 129 to 22, between 415 and 450 s.

And that blur is a focus pull, not the atmosphere. In the same frames a reed a few inches from the lens becomes sharper while the treeline behind it goes soft — crisp leaf edges and a bright line down the stem at t = 430, against a canopy that has lost its texture. Before and after the window the pattern reverses. No atmospheric effect can sharpen a near object while blurring a far one; only a lens racking focus forward does that. The autofocus was pulled to the near field, most plausibly by the newly-arrived, high-contrast, moving ripple texture directly in front of it.

So attempt three's disappearance carries two contributions on top of the range: the focus excursion, worth roughly 2.6× of the 5.4× contrast loss for a target unresolved in height, and near-field occlusion by the new chop, whose crests stand into a sightline beginning nine millimetres above the water.

He anticipates the focus objection in a caption — the trees come back into focus and the raft is still not there — and he is right about that. What it establishes is that the raft stayed invisible once sharpness returned. What it cannot establish is why it went, because by then it had been at maximum range for three and a half minutes and was at the edge of detectability regardless.

And the other two attempts had none of it

Which is the check that matters, because attempt three's event does not generalise.

Attempts one and two vanish over glassy water. No wind, no ripple trains, no near-field occluder, no focus excursion — continuous erect reflections run to the far bank in the very frames where the raft disappears. Yet the morphology is identical: an unresolved pale dot that dims smoothly in place over a couple of seconds and is gone, with no truncation edge and nothing rising through it. And in all three, the raft dies at the same place in the image — the compressed band where the far bank's last sliver, the waterline and the reflection fold pile into a few pixels.

The control is a white bench on the same bank. Sitting higher up the slope, it stays plainly visible in the very frames where the raft is lost. Whatever removes the raft is acting only at the waterline, not on the image as a whole — which rules out anything global, haze and exposure included, and points squarely back at the four-pixel pile-up above.

So the honest division: grazing-incidence compression and the detection floor operate in all three attempts and are the common cause. Attempt three adds a wind-driven focus pull and near-field chop on top. Refraction at mirage strength is excluded everywhere; curvature was never in it; and the book's angular-resolution mechanism cannot survive a raft that reappears whenever the camera is raised, since raising a camera does not change an object's angular size.

4 · What the footage cannot say

One limit is set by the camera rather than the pond, and it decides how much the footage could ever have shown. Matching the skyline between the uncropped run and the published frames puts the framing at the phone's telephoto end. At that framing the raft stands about 4 pixels tall, against a measured edge response — the smallest transition the delivered footage can render — of 9 pixels.

The raft's entire height is about half of the smallest edge this camera can draw.

A profile that short cannot display bottom-up truncation, because there is no room within it for a line to move up through. Worth being precise about what that does and does not mean: it does not make the disappearance unreal, and it does not mean the raft was invisible. It means the shape of the disappearance — cut off from below, versus faded out where it stood — was never something these frames could resolve on the raft itself. That is why §3 measures brightness instead, which the frames can resolve, and why §5d recommends a target tall enough that the question becomes readable.

Three further limits, stated plainly:

  • — We cannot put numbers on the shares. Compression, the detection floor, chop, near-field vegetation, the raft's own yaw and — in attempt three — the focus pull all act in the same direction and all were present. Naming the common cause is not the same as apportioning it, and this footage will not apportion it. Neither can we; neither can he.
  • — The verification clips were filmed after the run, not during it. Camera metadata puts the attempt at 11:07–11:19 and the “no obstruction” and “water is calm” checks at 11:32–11:40 — thirteen to twenty-one minutes later. On a sunny August morning, wind and the near-surface thermal profile both move on that timescale. The checks document the pond; they are not measurements of the conditions during the disappearance. This is an easy fix, not a fault.
  • — The distance-exponent test cannot be rescued from this run. We tried. Range recovery from the raft's apparent length fails once it is broken up by reeds and then unresolved, hidden height is not measurable at four pixels against a nine-to-twenty-two pixel blur, and the point-spread function moves 2.5× mid-run. It is the right test; it has to be designed in beforehand.

How coarse is this camera, actually?

There is a way to put that in units anybody can feel. Take the smallest transition the delivered footage can draw — nine pixels, at 0.533 arcminutes each — and read it as eyesight. It comes to 4.8 arcminutes. A person with 20/20 vision resolves about one.

At the framing he used, this video is watching the pond with something like 20/100 vision.

That is a strange thing to say about a modern phone, and it is worth being clear about why it is true. Vividness is contrast, colour and low noise, and an iPhone has all three in abundance — which is exactly why the footage is persuasive at the size most people watch it. What a small sensor behind a short lens does not have is angular resolution. Blown up to the scale this argument actually needs, the file is about five times coarser than the person holding it.

Set that against the target, keeping resolution and detection apart. The raft is 2.12′ tall and 37′ long, bright white on dark water. To a 1′ eye it is twice the limit in height and thirty-seven times in length — a sliver, but a comfortable one. To a 4.8′ camera the entire height fits inside half a blur width. That does not delete it, because an unresolved object still delivers all of its light; what it does is spread that light out, cutting peak contrast to roughly four-ninths of its true value and smearing what is left into the same few pixels as the waterline and the reflection fold beneath it. Losing the shape is the resolution limit. Losing the object is that dilution meeting the compressed band of §3.

Which leaves the book's own criterion in an awkward position. The one-arcminute threshold it argues from is an eye figure, and by that figure this target should never have been lost — twice the limit in height, thirty-seven times in length. The disappearance lives in the gap between a threshold defined for human vision and an instrument working about five times below it. A camera this coarse cannot test that criterion, in either direction.

The corollary cuts at us as much as at anyone. A person lying with one eye where that lens was would probably still have seen the raft in the frames where the camera shows nothing — probably, because that is an inference from acuity and contrast, not something anyone measured. And it is not easily checked, because no eye fits a third of an inch above a pond. That vantage exists only because a lens can be put where a head cannot. This is not a criticism of the method; extending the senses is what instruments are for. It does mean that every sentence of the form “look what you can see happen” is really “look what this camera at this framing renders” — and a 4.8-arcminute floor is doing heavy lifting inside that.

The same camera argument, from the other side

Anyone who has spent time in this debate has met the mirror image of this problem, and the two are worth setting side by side, because they are the same problem.

The genre is familiar: a skyline or an island that ought to be behind the curve, apparently missing; a Nikon P900 or P1000 comes out — eighty-three and a hundred and twenty-five times optical zoom; and the thing comes back. The video usually ends there. The instinct is a good one. When something vanishes, adding magnification to see whether it returns is a real test, and reaching for it is the right reflex. The difficulty is entirely in what gets read off the answer, because there are two quite different ways for something to be missing from a picture, and zoom treats them very differently.

  • — Unresolved. The light is arriving; the instrument is too coarse to lay it out. Focal length puts it back, every time. Nothing has been learned about the world.
  • — Blocked. Something stands in the light path — a crest, a stem, a hull below the horizon. No magnification recovers it; a bigger lens only shows the obstruction more clearly.
What zoom brings back was never hidden, only unresolved — a fact about the camera. What zoom cannot bring back is physically in the way — a fact about the world. That is a genuinely useful instrument, and here it is available for the price of an afternoon. It is also, on the published files, the one test this experiment has not had: every zoomed shot in the video is from a raised camera, where the geometry has already changed. Nobody has yet magnified this disappearance from the height at which it happens.

And the symmetry has to be granted in both directions or it is not worth having. Footage shot low over water with a long lens on the globe side inherits exactly the same two limits — its own instrument floor and the near-surface air — and hull-down video that ignores them deserves the scrutiny this page is giving the pond. What distinguishes the ship case is not who filmed it. It is that it survives the two tests this run cannot: the truncation is resolved, a sharp cut with a sharp survivor standing above it, and magnification does not undo it. Those are checkable claims about footage, which is the only kind worth arguing about.

5 · How to make this decisive

This is the part worth the reader's attention, and it is offered in the spirit the experiment was run in. The pond test as it stands establishes that something hid the raft. Five changes would establish what, and four of them are an afternoon's work at the same pond.

None of what follows is new, and that is the point. The Bedford Level experiment was run the same way in 1838 — telescope eight inches above the water — and settled in 1870 when Alfred Russel Wallace redid it with two changes: he raised the sightline to thirteen feet, out of the near-surface layer, and he added a marker at the midpoint so the geometry could be read rather than argued. Both of those are below, as §5b and §5d. The full account is in §6 of Bottom-Up Observations. We are not proposing a novel dodge; we are proposing the correction that resolved this exact experimental design a century and a half ago.

5a · Zoom in, from exactly where you are

Before moving anything, add focal length. Same lens height, same raft, same 540 feet — a superzoom bridge camera, or a phone behind a spotting scope, on the tripod, held through a disappearance.

The requirement is modest. At the framing he used the raft stands four pixels against a nine-pixel edge; about 270 mm equivalent brings it to parity with the camera's own blur, and about 580 mm brings the whole instrument down to what an eye does. A P900 reaches 2,000 mm. This is not an equipment problem.

And it falls cleanly whichever way it goes. If the raft comes back, it was never hidden — only unresolved — and the disappearance is a fact about the camera rather than about the pond. If it stays gone under five times the magnification, something is physically in the light path, and the question narrows to what and how high, which the graduated staff of §5d then answers. Either way the largest ambiguity on this page closes, for one afternoon and no new geometry.

It is the cheapest of the five and it moves the fewest variables, which is why it comes first. It is also the one test both sides of this argument already trust.

5b · Raise everything six feet, and change nothing else

Put the camera on a tripod at six feet and the target on a stand at six feet, same 540-foot separation, same pond, same day. The point is that the candidate mechanisms disagree sharply about what should happen, and one of them disagrees with all the others.

MechanismWhat it predicts when both ends rise six feet
Earth's curvatureNo change. Horizon moves to about three miles; 540 ft is still nothing. Hidden height stays zero.
The raft's angular size, against any resolution limitNo change. It still subtends 2.12′. Raising both ends together changes the slant range by about 0.05 mm in 540 ft — one part in three and a half million.
Grazing-incidence compressionEffect disappears. The water behind the raft opens from 2′ to 374′, so the target stops being buried in a four-pixel pile-up at the horizon. This is the row that makes the test less decisive than it looks — see below.
The book's vanishing-point argumentEffect should disappear. Its own claim is that raising the observer lets you see more. The angle between the raft's base and eye level goes from 0.199′ to 38.2′ — a 192-fold increase — so on this reading the raft should become dramatically easier to see.
Near-surface refractionEffect is excluded, not merely weakened. The strong gradient lives in the lowest metre or so; at six feet the ray is clear of it for the whole path. Burying the same four inches from a six-foot lens would need light to curve 1,300 times more sharply than the Earth does — a gradient of 237 °C per metre, which is out of the question.
Ripple occlusionEffect disappears. The lever arm collapses once the sightline clears the crests: a half-inch wave fifteen feet out cannot occlude anything at all from a six-foot lens.

This test is worth running, but it is not the clean split it looks like. Everything in the lower half of that table — refraction, ripples, reeds, compression, and the book's own vanishing-point argument — predicts the same thing when you raise the camera: the effect weakens or dies. So a raft that reappears at six feet tells you the cause lives close to the water, which is worth establishing, but it does not say which cause. And a raft that still vanishes from six feet would be the more interesting result, because it kills all of them at once and leaves only a plain contrast-detection limit — a fact about cameras and backgrounds, not about the shape of the Earth.

Separating the low-level candidates from one another needs a different instrument, and §5d is it. Compression cannot bury a tall target: it crowds the lowest couple of inches into the horizon band and leaves everything above plainly visible. Refraction of the strength this would require puts a genuine hiding line that climbs with range. A graduated staff reads the difference directly — how much is hidden, and whether that amount grows with distance. Repeating at dawn is a second, free discriminator: pure geometry gives an identical result at any hour, while a thermal layer should change or reverse.

One confound to control while doing it: at 0.375 in the raft is seen against distant water, and at six feet against water at a quite different grazing angle, possibly against the far bank. Background luminance is the dominant term in whether anything is detected at all, so the two heights need the same background — which is another reason to film them simultaneously, as §5e recommends, and to report the background brightness in each.

One honest caveat on our own prediction: the near-surface gradient does not switch off at some height, it weakens. Expect the effect to drop by a large factor at six feet, not to vanish to precisely zero. If the raft becomes markedly harder to lose but can still be coaxed away, that is informative too — and it is a result no amount of arguing produces.

5c · Then put a board in the way, deliberately

The proposal that goes with the six-foot test is a level board set in front of the target as an artificial horizon — and it is a good idea, though we would frame what it does slightly differently. It is not really a test of the mechanism. It is a calibrated hiding line at a known height, and what it answers is the question this whole dispute keeps running into:

What does four inches of genuine, deliberate occlusion actually look like through this camera, at this range?

Nobody knows, because it has never been filmed. Set the board to cut off a known amount of the target — two inches, say — and shoot it at the same framing. Two things come out at once. First, whether bottom-up truncation is resolvable at all at four pixels of target height: if a real board doing real hiding cannot produce a visible cut line, then no pond footage at that framing could ever have shown one. Second, the morphology of true occlusion, to hold against the fade: a hiding edge should give a sharp cut with the surviving top at full brightness, which is emphatically not what the photometry above shows.

5d · Swap the raft for something you can read

This is the single biggest improvement available, and it costs a plank of wood. The reason the run cannot be analysed further is that the target is four inches tall and the camera's smallest resolvable transition is about nine pixels against a raft height of four — so how much was hidden was never a measurable quantity. It was only ever visible or not visible.

Replace the lounger with a vertical staff, three feet tall, painted in two-inch bands, alternating high-contrast. Now the observable is a number: the lowest band still visible. A pole is also rigid, symmetric and of constant cross-section, which removes the yaw and wedge problems above at the same time. Hidden height can be read directly, at every distance, on every frame, without fitting anything. This is not a novel idea — it is what Wallace used on the Bedford Level in 1870, and it is why that experiment produced a result while Rowbotham's produced an argument.

With a readable staff, the decisive measurement becomes available: how hidden height grows with distance. The three mechanisms separate cleanly, because each has its own exponent in H = e + A·dn:

MechanismExponent n
A near obstruction (crest, reeds)≈ 0.9 — close to linear
A uniform refracting layer≈ 1.8
Earth's curvature≈ 2.6 — and not reachable inside 4,876 ft

Walk the staff out in marked stages, read the lowest visible band at each, plot log H against log d. The slope names the mechanism. That is a real experiment with a real discriminator, which is the same compliment we paid the equator-flight challenge and meant just as literally.

5e · Two cameras, one instant — and lock them down

Two smaller changes that remove most of what is currently unarguable.

  • — Film both heights simultaneously. One phone at 0.375 in, one at six feet, same target, same second, ideally on the same tripod mast. This is what makes the comparison in §5b airtight: filming the two heights twenty minutes apart leaves wind, surface state and the thermal profile free to change in between, which is the same objection that applies to the verification clips.
  • — Lock focus, exposure and zoom, on a tripod. The autofocus excursion documented above is the single most damaging technical problem with the current run, and on an iPhone it is a long press to defeat. Also state the zoom used — the whole imaging argument on this page had to be reconstructed by matching skylines because the framing was never given.
  • — Two thermometers and a wind reading. One at 1 cm above the water, one at 50 cm, plus water temperature. Under a pound. The footage already excludes a layer strong enough to bury the raft (§3a), but it cannot exclude a weak one contributing a fraction of an inch, and two thermometers turn that from an open question into a number.
And one free improvement suggested by a commenter, which is better than anything above: run it again in winter, on a frozen lake. Ice removes the ripples and the near-shore vegetation outright, and water near freezing removes the superadiabatic case. If the disappearance survives that, the local explanations are in real trouble — and it would be the strongest version of this experiment anyone has filmed.

6 · What would change our mind

Stated in advance, because that is the standard this review has asked of the book and owes in return.

  • — A raft that still vanishes from six feet up, filmed simultaneously against a ground-level camera, with focus locked. That would kill ripples, reeds and near-surface refraction in one shot, and we would say so on this page.
  • — A hidden-height exponent near 2.6 from a graduated staff over a decent range of distances. We do not expect it inside 4,876 feet, and it would be a genuine surprise.
  • — A raft that stays gone under five times the magnification, from the same lens height, with focus locked. That would put something physically in the light path and leave the compression account insufficient on its own, and we would have to go looking again.

What would not change our mind is a repeat of the same run in 4K, or at a higher bitrate. That is a different thing from §5a: magnification at capture genuinely buys angular resolution, whereas a cleaner encode of the same two-arcminute framing mostly buys a sharper picture of the same blur. And the deeper problem is not the camera at all; it is that four inches of target at 540 feet cannot distinguish between five mechanisms that all point the same way. Better pixels do not fix an underdetermined design. A readable staff and a second camera height do.

The last word should be the fair one. He built a rig, checked his own arithmetic, published the raw files, and invited criticism — and the arithmetic held up under it. Most of what is wrong with the run is wrong with every first attempt at a field measurement, and the fixes above are the ones any experimentalist would reach for second time round. We would rather see the second run than win the argument about the first.
