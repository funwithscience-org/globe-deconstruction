Rockets Don't Push Against Air

Answering Shape Debate's Claim #2 — that Newton's third law can't apply to a rocket in vacuum, because thrust needs something to push off.

Claim #2 on shapedebate.com argues that mainstream physics misapplies Newton's third law to pressurized gas release in vacuum: “thrust must have something to push off, and an infinite vacuum does not provide that,” and that if this were tested and confirmed, it would show the law was “abused to sell humanity on the idea of space travel.” The page points to a pending vacuum-chamber test by Alex Kampf, “Propulsion Tester,” results not yet uploaded. The short version: the physics claim is backwards on its own terms — the rocket thrust equation predicts more thrust as ambient pressure drops, not less — and the test being proposed isn't hypothetical. It's been run, in exactly the failure mode the claim worries about, for over sixty years, at industrial scale, with the receipts still visible in every orbital launch's own telemetry.

What the claim gets backwards

“Thrust needs a medium to push against” describes a propeller or a jet engine, not a rocket. A rocket's third-law pair is the rocket and its own exhaust — mass it carries with it and throws overboard — not the rocket and the surrounding air. That's not an assumption smoothed over for public consumption; it's the same equation aerospace engineers use to size every nozzle ever flown, and it says thrust goes up, not to zero, as ambient pressure falls.

1 · The claim, stated fairly

Quoting the site directly: “Mainstream physics is completely wrong in applying Newton's third law to a pressurized gas release in a vacuum environment. Thrust must have something to push off, and an infinite vacuum does not provide that. If verified, this implies the law was abused to sell humanity on the idea of space travel.” The page also flags, in its own words, that a common way this kind of test goes wrong is using a vacuum chamber that's too small — worth taking seriously, and returned to in §6 below. As of this writing the referenced experiment by Alex Kampf is listed as pending upload, so this page argues from the claim as stated and from the physics and public telemetry already available, not from a result that doesn't exist yet.

2 · What's worth conceding up front

The chamber-size caution is real, and it's good instinct to flag it before running the test rather than after. A vacuum chamber that's small relative to the thrust and burn duration will re-pressurize from the exhaust itself faster than it can be pumped down, especially with a valve or piston releasing compressed gas rather than a sustained combustion exhaust plume. A test like that can look like thrust “fails” in vacuum when what actually failed was the vacuum. That's a legitimate methodological trap, and it's exactly the trap that real propulsion labs are built to avoid — see §6.

3 · Newton's third law doesn't ask what's outside the rocket

The third-law pair is the rocket and its own exhaust. The vacuum is irrelevant to the pairing.

Momentum conservation applies to any two masses that interact by mutual force — it says nothing about what else is or isn't present around them. A rocket engine burns propellant it is carrying, accelerates the combustion products out through a nozzle at high velocity, and by conservation of momentum the rocket itself accelerates the other way. The exhaust doesn't need anything to bounce off; it is the reaction mass. That's why the standard classroom demonstrations for “does this need air” are things like a fire extinguisher recoiling, a person in a wheeled chair throwing a weight, or gun recoil — none of them require, or even benefit from, a surrounding medium.

The actual rocket thrust equation used by aerospace engineers makes this quantitative:

F = ṁ·Ve + Ae(pe − p0)

where ṁ is the propellant mass flow rate, Ve the exhaust velocity, Ae the nozzle exit area, pe the exhaust pressure at the nozzle exit, and p0 the ambient (outside) pressure. The first term — momentum thrust — is present whether p0 is one atmosphere or zero; it's pure Newton's third law and doesn't reference the surroundings at all. The second term is a pressure-difference correction, and as p0 falls toward vacuum, (pe − p0) grows, adding thrust rather than removing it. A real engine's exhaust also expands more completely and efficiently with nothing pushing back on it, which is a second, independent reason vacuum thrust ratings are consistently higher than sea-level ratings for the same engine. Both terms move the same direction, and it's the opposite direction from what the claim predicts.

The real curve keeps climbing as the rocket empties out and the air thins — peaking hardest right before engine cutoff, in air thin enough to be a fraction of a percent of sea level. An “air pusher” engine, built on exactly the same fuel schedule, would collapse toward zero thrust over the same stretch and the vehicle would fall back to Earth rather than reach orbital speed. Those aren't two competing interpretations of ambiguous data; they're two sharply different, easily distinguishable predictions, and every successful orbital launch is a repeated, public test of which one actually happens.

5 · Independent of thrust mechanism: the air is functionally gone long before it would matter

Even setting the thrust-mechanism question aside, “no medium to push against” isn't some exotic condition unique to a vacuum chamber or deep space — it's the normal state of the sky well below where second-stage burns and orbital insertion happen. The chart below extends the same atmospheric model up past the Kármán line (100 km, the conventional edge of space) to low Earth orbit altitudes like the ISS (~423 km).

Pressure at 80 km is already roughly 0.0006% of sea level; by the Kármán line it's immeasurably close to the pressure of interplanetary space. If a rocket's thrust genuinely depended on ambient air, it would have already failed by the time a Falcon 9 second stage lights, tens of kilometres below where anyone claims spacecraft do their real work. The claim would predict engine failure at an altitude where every orbital mission on record is still climbing normally.

6 · The vacuum-chamber test isn't hypothetical — it's been run for sixty years, at a scale built to avoid exactly the pitfall flagged in §2

NASA Glenn's Electric Propulsion and Power Laboratory has been testing thrusters in hard vacuum since the 1960s. Its largest chamber, Vacuum Facility 5, is 4.6 m in diameter and 18.3 m long, pumped down to roughly 1×10−7 torr — a size and pumping capacity specifically chosen so a firing thruster's own exhaust can't re-pressurize the chamber faster than it's evacuated, which is precisely the failure mode the chamber-size caution in §2 is worried about. Ion and Hall-effect thrusters (including NASA's NEXT engine) have been hot-fired there and at comparable facilities worldwide for thousands of hours, in real vacuum, with thrust measured directly on a stand — not inferred, not simulated.

Every one of those tests, run by multiple independent space agencies and companies over decades, shows sustained, steady thrust in vacuum — not the collapse-to-zero the claim predicts. So has every second-stage and on-orbit engine restart ever telemetered, and every deep-space probe (Voyager, New Horizons, both Pioneers) that fired trajectory-correction thrusters in interplanetary vacuum, millions of kilometres from any atmosphere at all.

7 · Where we're genuinely willing to wait and see

In fairness: a new, independently run vacuum-chamber test isn't a bad idea just because the physics is already well established elsewhere — replication is a fine instinct, and Shape Debate's own page correctly names the single most common way an amateur version of this test goes wrong. What actually determines whether the Kampf test lands as evidence either way is whether the chamber and pump-down are large enough, relative to the thrust and burn duration used, that the chamber pressure stays low throughout the firing rather than rising as exhaust accumulates. That's a checkable, falsifiable detail once the results are up: if thrust holds steady while the chamber stays at a documented low pressure throughout the burn, that confirms standard physics; if thrust collapses while the chamber pressure is simultaneously rising, that shows a chamber-sizing problem, not a Newton's-third-law problem.

The honest bottom line

The claim's own physical intuition — “thrust needs something to push off” — correctly describes propellers and jet engines, which really do stop working without air. It just doesn't describe rockets, whose reaction mass is the propellant they carry, not the surrounding atmosphere. The rocket thrust equation used to size real engines predicts thrust rising, not falling, as ambient pressure drops. Real Falcon 9 telemetry shows exactly that rise, all the way to engine cutoff in air thinner than a rounding error. And the vacuum-chamber test being proposed isn't a thought experiment — it's been run, at a scale specifically built to dodge the small-chamber trap the claim itself flags, for over sixty years, with the same result every time.
