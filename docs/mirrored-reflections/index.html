    The Mirror Is 125 Metres Long, and It Settles Nothing

    Answering Q4 (p.47) and the reflection chapter (pp.113-116) of Globe Deconstruction? - including two corrections to our own arithmetic.

    Unreviewed first draft. Published for review only, not linked as an answer in the catalogue, marked noindex.
    • Rebuilt from a draft that was held back. The geometry was recomputed — the reflecting strip is 41.0 to 166.2 m, not the flat-plane 37.4 to 119.8 m — and a quotation under an explicit publication hold was removed. The checking pass found no critical failures in this version.
    Treat any figure as provisional until this notice is removed.

    Levi Miller asks whether perfectly mirrored reflections survive over large bodies of water as observer height increases, and proposes a drone experiment at Lake Pukaki to settle it. We grant his optics, we grant his site figures, and we grant that his central expectation is correct: on a globe, reflection distortion does grow with observer height. What we set out to establish was how much, and where the mirror doing the work actually sits. Solving the equal-angle condition on a sphere of radius 6,371 km, the reflected image of a mountain 3,206 m above the lake at 60 km, seen by an eye 2 m above the water, is assembled from a strip running from 41.0 m to 166.2 m in front of the observer: 125.2 m of water, whose greatest departure from the chord joining its ends is 0.308 mm. The lake's eighteen miles never enter the optics at that vantage. Our conclusion is a null, and it cuts both ways. The drone version of the test can discriminate, but only with a temperature profile taken alongside it.

    Where this lands

    At shore level the reflection argument does not test Earth's shape. The mirror forming the image is about 125 metres of water, not 29 kilometres, and across 125 metres the two models differ by a fraction of a millimetre - far below the slope of a capillary ripple. That disposes of the claim. It equally disposes of the reply sometimes offered on our own side, that a mirror-flat lake demonstrates curvature: it does not, and nobody should say it does. The photograph is consistent with both models and discriminates between neither. The question Miller actually asks - does distortion increase with observer height - is a different and better one, and the answer is yes. That measurement can discriminate, but only if a near-surface temperature profile is taken at the same time, because a dawn inversion over cold water can produce a compression of the same size and sign that curvature predicts. Without the thermometers the drone experiment is not a test of Earth's shape; with them it is, and we will treat its result as one. We also correct two errors of our own: our reflecting strip was 52 per cent too short, and our headline compression figure came from a band of the mountain our own protocol says is not observable.

    What the claim is

    Q4, on p.47, is one sentence: "Do we observe perfectly mirrored reflections over large bodies of water at rest as we increase observer height?" It is carried by the chapter on pp.113-116, headed "How optics are abused to fake curvature."

    The chapter states the law of reflection correctly, in two parts, with a correct diagram; shows a photograph of the ephemeral lake at Badwater Basin returning a mirror image of the range beyond it; poses a challenge on p.115 - "Are you able to produce a continuous, straight line of reflection from the Sun or Moon to the observer over a curved reflective surface?"; and makes a proposal on p.116: Lake Pukaki, eighteen miles long, Aoraki/Mount Cook at 12,218 ft, a drone flown at several heights, and a later computer comparison of a globe model against a topographic plane, with the expectation stated plainly - "If the Earth is a globe, we should see greater reflection distortion as the observer's height increases."

    Two separable arguments sit inside that: mirror images of a fixed object on water at rest (pp.113-114, p.116), and the Sun's or Moon's reflection as a continuous streak (p.115). They require opposite surface states, so we answer them separately. We reviewed only those book pages; we could not access shapedebate.com, and if a fuller version lives there we have not seen it.

    What it gets right, at length

    The two rules of reflection on pp.113-114 are correct, both of them. Every result below is obtained by applying them more carefully. Nothing below contradicts them.

    His site figures are right and current. Aoraki/Mount Cook stands at 3,724 m, which is 12,218.5 ft - the post-2013 surveyed height from the University of Otago School of Surveying's AORAKI2013 project, down from the 3,754 m set after the 1991 rockfall. His 12,218 ft is the current value, not a rounded legacy one. Lake Pukaki's surface sits at 518.2 m above sea level at normal operating level, putting the summit 3,206 m above the water. His "eighteen miles" is consistent with the 18.30 miles we measure from map coordinates. Badwater Basin is a well-chosen example too: the shallow ephemeral lake that formed there in 2023-24 genuinely produced the reflections he shows.

    Calm water really does return essentially undistorted mirror images of distant mountains, and the globe predicts exactly that. Our ray trace on a sphere of radius 6,371 km, for an eye 2 m above the lake and the mountain band from 1,000 m to 3,206 m at 60 km, gives a reflected image whose internal vertical scale is 0.9989 of the direct image: 0.11 per cent of compression, about 0.13 arcminutes spread across a band 2.104 degrees tall. Nothing at that framing resolves it. He is right that shore-level reflections look perfect, and we agree that they should.

    And the prediction that matters: his expectation that reflection distortion should increase with observer height is correct. It is what spherical geometry gives. He has identified a real, computable signature and has not yet computed it - nor, as far as we can find, has anyone else.

    This page exists to supply the number he is missing, and then to explain why the observation he already has cannot carry the argument in either direction.

    Where the mirror actually is

    The chapter's title implies that a curved reflecting surface cannot give a clean mirror image. That premise is never stated outright, and it is where the argument goes wrong. Convex mirrors form images - virtual, erect and demagnified, in every shop security dome and every wing mirror - and finding the reflection point on one is an old, settled problem, posed as Alhazen's problem in Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics around 1021. The only question is how much demagnification, which depends on the mirror's radius compared with the distances involved.

    Those distances are not the lake's. Each point of the object has one reflection point: where the surface normal bisects the directions to object and observer. Find those points and you have found the mirror. The rest is scenery.

    Set the eye 2 m above the water, put Aoraki's summit 3,206 m above the water at 60 km, and solve the equal-angle condition on a sphere of radius 6,371 km. Earth's sag over the 60 km leg is 60,000 squared / (2 x 6,371,000) = 282.5 m; measured from the reflection point it is slightly less, and the summit stands 2,923.9 m above the tangent plane there. The specular point then sits at 60,000 x 2 / (2 + 2,923.9) = 41.0 m from the observer. Repeat for the mountain's 1,000 m contour - which loses a far larger share of its height to that same sag - and the exact spherical solve puts its specular point at 166.2 m.

    So the reflected image of the upper mountain is assembled from water lying between 41.0 m and 166.2 m from the photographer's feet. The reflecting strip is 125.2 m long.

    The flat-plane shortcut fails badly here. Solved on a plane instead of a sphere, the same strip comes out as 37.4 m to 119.8 m, a span of 82.4 m. That is wrong by 9.6 per cent at the summit end, 39 per cent at the far end, and 52 per cent in the strip's length — so the usual assurance that "the spherical correction is under 10 per cent" does not hold in this geometry, and the exact solve is not optional.

    Now the flatness of that strip, named precisely, because several quantities get called "flatness" and give different answers for the same water. We use the chord sagitta: the greatest departure of the surface from the straight line joining its ends. Over 125.2 m that is 125.2 squared / (8 x 6,371,000) = 0.308 mm.

    The chord sagitta is 0.308 mm. It is a different quantity from the drop of the strip's far end below the horizontal plane through its near end, which over the same 125.2 m is 2.04 mm. Both are true; they measure different things; we quote the sagitta throughout. A bare "flat to a fraction of a millimetre" is ambiguous between the two and should not be used by either side.

    The comparison that decides the section is slope, not sag. Across 125.2 m the surface normal turns by 125.2 / 6,371,000 = 1.97 x 10^-5 radians, or 0.00113 degrees. A facet tilted by delta deflects the ray by twice delta, so holding the image sharp to one arcminute requires facet slopes below half an arcminute, 0.00833 degrees - a tolerance 7.4 times larger than the total slope change curvature imposes across the whole strip.

    Earth's curvature across the mirror that forms the reflection is about seven times smaller than the surface roughness the sharp reflection already survives, and a millimetre-high capillary ripple exceeds it outright.

    Which is why the length of Lake Pukaki never enters the optics of a shore-level photograph. Whatever the lake measures, the mirror is 125 metres of it.

    Why the shore-level observation is a null, in both directions

    Having found the mirror and measured its departure from flat, the natural next move is to declare the claim answered. That is not quite what the arithmetic gives. What it establishes is that the shore-level observation carries no information about Earth's shape. Over the reflecting strip, on a globe, the surface departs from its own chord by three tenths of a millimetre and turns through about a thousandth of a degree. On a plane it departs by zero and turns by zero. Those two mirrors produce images differing by far less than the slope of one capillary ripple.

    So the photograph is consistent with a globe. It is also consistent with a plane. It cannot distinguish them, and no amount of care in taking it will change that, because the difference sits below the water's own noise floor.

    The mirror-flat lake photograph is not weak evidence for either model. It is no evidence for either model. The two predictions are identical to well within the roughness of real water.

    That cuts in the direction the claim runs. It cuts equally in the other. The reply sometimes offered on our own side - that a lake returning a perfect mirror image demonstrates curvature, or shows water lying level on a curved Earth - is the same error pointed the other way, and this page is against it as firmly as it is against the claim. A perfect reflection is a statement about wave slopes and about where the mirror is, not about the shape of the Earth.

    A page saying "this observation settles nothing, and here is the arithmetic showing why" is less satisfying than one saying "this observation is wrong". It is the true one. The shore-level reflection is not bad evidence. It is not evidence at all.

    What rises with the observer, and why the number depends on which two features you pick

    Q4 asks about observer height, and there the answer changes. As the eye rises the specular point walks away from it, the strip lengthens, and the patches forming the top and bottom of the image tilt appreciably relative to one another. Since a facet tilted by delta turns the ray by twice delta, the image is compressed vertically by roughly twice the spread of patch tilts - intuition rather than the result, and it drifts at height (0.9103 against a traced 0.9239 at 400 m), but it shows why the effect exists.

    The consequence our earlier draft missed is this. The compression is not a property of the mountain. It is a property of the two features you measure between. The specular point for a feature at height h sits near D x h_o / (h_o + h), so the spread is dominated by the lower of the two: move the pair up the mountain and it shrinks quickly; let the lower feature reach toward the base and it stretches. Across the pairs a photographer could plausibly identify, the band-averaged compression varies by more than a factor of two.

    Which pairs are observable therefore decides the number. A mountain's base at 60 km is usually obscured - by intervening ranges, by haze, by the far shore - and our own protocol below says to avoid it. The 1,000 m to summit band, which our earlier draft used for its headline, includes exactly the terrain that protocol excludes.

    Within that band the compression grows from 0.11 per cent at the shore to 4.2 per cent at 120 m. But 4.2 per cent is not the number to look for. For a pair of features both high on the mountain - the observable case - our trace gives about 1.8 per cent at 120 m. That is the figure this page stands behind, roughly half of what we published before. Against it a plane predicts 0.03 per cent or less across the same heights: a factor of sixty or more between the models, at an altitude Miller has already proposed flying.

    Refraction shrinks the signature without erasing it. Re-running the full-band trace with the substitution R_eff = R/(1-k) gives, at 120 m, 0.9643 at k = 0.13, 0.9662 at k = 0.17 and 0.9722 even at k = 0.30 - a reduction of about a fifth. The plane's prediction does not move.

    So the answer to Q4 as asked is yes, and its useful form is a function rather than a number: too small to see from the shore for any choice of features, and just large enough to see from a drone if the features are chosen high and named.

    Curvature does not hide a reflection while leaving the object

    One further consequence of the same geometry. A specular path exists only if the reflection point is visible from both object and observer, and tracing the clearance of both legs above the sphere, the reflected path is obstructed at exactly the object height at which the direct path is: for an eye 2 m up at 20 km, both cross at 17.545 m. Object and reflection appear and disappear together, and as the object recedes they converge and merge - for a 20 m target seen from 2 m, their angular separation falls to 0.00008 degrees at 21.0 km, where the geometric sum of the two horizon distances is 21.012 km. This is the ordinary sight of a distant hull meeting its own reflection at the waterline.

    The identity of cut-offs is a straight-ray result, and we state the domain with the rule. Under a strong inversion the reflected leg runs lower through the temperature profile than the direct leg and the two can decouple. Over water at dawn that exception is not rare.

    The glitter path is a wave phenomenon, not a curvature phenomenon

    p.115 asks for a continuous straight line of reflection from the Sun or Moon over a curved surface. The difficulty is not curvature. It is that a perfectly smooth surface cannot produce a line of reflection at all: on mirror-calm water the Sun's reflection is one image of the Sun, a disc about 0.53 degrees across. Shaw and Churnside put it in a sentence: "A perfectly smooth surface would contain only one glint." The streak everyone has seen is a glitter path, thousands of separate glints off wave facets whose slopes happen at that moment to be right, and its existence is direct evidence that the water is not at rest. Its geometry follows from slope statistics rather than Earth's shape: angular length about four times the maximum wave slope, width-to-length ratio the sine of the source's elevation. Cox and Munk measured that distribution in 1954 by photographing this pattern from an aircraft, and their relation - mean square slope 0.003 + 0.00512 W, W in m/s at 12.5 m - is still the standard, quoted verbatim in NASA's ICESat-2 ATL13 Algorithm Theoretical Basis Document.

    Along the glade the required facet tilt runs from zero at the mirror-specular point up to half the source's elevation far out; on a sphere, curvature adds half the dip angle at the far end. From an eye 2 m up the dip is 2.72 arcminutes, so curvature contributes 1.36 arcminutes, against a dead-calm Cox-Munk RMS slope of about 188 arcminutes - under one per cent of one standard deviation of the roughness that creates the path.

    Cox and Munk is calibrated on the open ocean; a sheltered alpine lake at dawn lies outside that calibration, and a mirror-sharp reflection implies RMS slopes below about 0.01 degrees. We use it as a ceiling, not a model.

    Curvature does one thing here, and it is falsifiable in one direction. On a globe the glade cannot extend past the horizon: 5.05 km geometric and 5.54 km at k = 0.17 from an eye 2 m up, 39.1 km and 42.9 km from 120 m. On an unbounded plane it runs to the water's edge. So a photograph of a continuous glitter path reaching a shore demonstrably beyond the observer's horizon distance, with that shore in frame, is a direct problem for the globe. The caveat comes immediately: slope statistics normally fade the glade out well before the horizon, so a short glade proves nothing.

    One tension follows, noted without reading anything into it: pp.113-114 describe a mirror, p.115 asks for a streak, and the same water cannot be in both states at once.

    The measurements that already exist, made for other reasons

    The specular geometry of a curved water surface is not something anyone had to invent for this debate. It is used by people with other reasons to care whether it is right.

    Sentinel-3's OLCI instrument has its field of view tilted 12.6 degrees west of nadir specifically to dodge sun glint - a change from MERIS, made because glint "affects almost half of the MERIS observations at sub-tropical latitudes". The glint region is predicted from specular geometry on an ellipsoidal Earth plus wave-slope statistics, and the tilt was sized from that prediction. GNSS reflectometry does the same computation explicitly: the Haleakalā mountaintop experiment computes "the specular reflection point on the WGS84 ellipsoid" and adds transmitter-to-point and point-to-receiver ranges, needing that geometry good to half a chip - roughly 150 m at L1CA/L2C. That is the lake case at radio wavelengths, solved operationally.

    Radar link engineering carries a divergence factor whose whole content is that the reflecting surface is convex: "when an electromagnetic wave is incident on a round earth surface, the reflected wave diverges because of the earth's curvature... the reflected energy is defocused, and the radar power density is reduced." We quote that sentence and no numbers with it, having computed illustrative decibel values and then found two readings of the same secondary text disagreeing on the algebra, with Kerr (1951) beyond our reach.

    The brightness of these grazing reflections, meanwhile, is a laboratory constant of water: Fresnel reflectance at n = 1.333 runs 2.04 per cent at normal incidence but 89.6 per cent at 1 degree grazing, and the summit's grazing angle at our corrected specular point is 2.79 degrees. Near water's Brewster angle of 53.12 degrees a filter can nearly extinguish a reflection, so how perfect a reflection looks is partly an instrument property.

    The test, specified

    He proposed the right experiment, and it deserves a usable version. Same lens, same session, same target, inside a single hour. Pick two identifiable features on the mountain - a summit and a named col, not the base, which is usually obscured and whose inclusion inflated our own earlier figure. State which two features they are and what their elevations are, because the prediction depends on that choice by more than a factor of two. Measure the angular separation between them in the direct image and in the reflected image, and take the ratio. Repeat at eye height, 10 m, 30 m, 60 m and 120 m, the New Zealand visual-line-of-sight ceiling.

    Predictions: a plane holds the ratio at 0.9997 or better across that whole range. A sphere takes it down by about 1.8 per cent at 120 m for a high pair, and by up to 4.2 per cent for a pair reaching down to 1,000 m. Standard refraction reduces those by roughly a fifth.

    Run air temperature at 0.1 m, 1 m and 10 m above the water at the same time. That is not garnish. It is the experiment.

    Publish the frames unretouched, the feature elevations, and the ratios with their uncertainties. At 120 m the full-band model difference is about 5.33 arcminutes across a 2.104-degree band, some 18 pixels on a 4,000-pixel frame covering 20 degrees vertically. A high pair gives less, so the measurement needs care.

    He proposed a measurement nobody appears to have made. The right response is a protocol, not a verdict.

    What would change our mind

    Stated in advance, and specifically.

    A Lake Pukaki drone sequence - same lens, same hour, eye level through 120 m - showing the reflected-to-direct ratio for two identified features flat to within plus or minus 0.5 per cent across the whole range. A plane predicts a change under 0.03 per cent; a sphere predicts a fall of order one to four per cent depending on the pair. A careful null at that precision falsifies the spherical geometry at that site, and we will say so here.

    A photograph, with the observer's height stated, of a continuous glitter path reaching a far shore demonstrably beyond the horizon distance for that height - 5.05 km geometric, 5.54 km refracted, from 2 m - with that shore visible at the glade's far end.

    Any instance, over still water at one moment, of a reflected image showing more of an object than the direct view does. Our trace says both are cut off at the same object height, 17.545 m for a 2 m eye at 20 km. A documented counter-example, with a temperature profile ruling out a mirage, would mean our reflection geometry is wrong.

    A measured near-surface temperature profile showing refraction genuinely negligible - absolute k below 0.05 through the lowest 10 m - alongside a drone sequence showing compression substantially larger than the k = 0 prediction. Something would then be operating that neither model contains.

    A bench demonstration on a large convex mirror, its radius scaled so that object and observer distances match the lake case, showing the image fail qualitatively rather than be demagnified by the computed amount.

    A primary-source derivation showing our specular-point solution or effective-radius substitution wrong at first order. Our scripts are reproducible and we will publish them; a corrected trace changing the 120 m prediction by more than a factor of two changes our answer to Q4.

    Where this page could be wrong

    We have corrected two pieces of our own work here, which is reason enough to be explicit about the rest.

    The two corrections. The reflecting strip was given as 82.4 m and is 125.2 m; we extended a spherical result with a flat-plane formula and excused it as "under 10 per cent" when the error at the far end was 39 per cent. And we headlined 4.2 per cent of compression at 120 m, from a band running down to the 1,000 m contour, while our own protocol on the same page said to avoid the base as unobservable; the observable figure is about 1.8 per cent. Both failures were of one kind: a number carried forward without re-checking that the case it came from was the case being described. The compression is a prediction, not a result. We could find no published measurement of height-dependent compression of a reflected image over water. Nothing here reports an observation, and the 1.8 per cent must not be quoted as one. We also do not quote reflecting-strip endpoints at drone altitudes: those earlier values came from the same flat-plane formula that failed at eye level, and we have not recomputed them on a sphere. Refraction may be the same size as the signal, and this is the strongest objection to our own experiment. Mirror-calm water occurs at dawn in still air, when the strongest and least standard near-surface inversions form over water. The reflected ray runs lower through that profile than the direct ray, so refraction acts on the two unequally and can compress the reflected image on its own. A duct over a cold lake could produce several per cent of differential compression, larger than the 1.8 per cent we propose to measure. Our k = 0 to 0.30 band is a sensitivity study, not a bound, and R_eff = R/(1-k) is a first-order device, not a rigorous treatment of a reflection through a stratified atmosphere. Several site figures are our own map work. The lake length of 29.45 km (18.30 miles) and the shore-to-summit distances - 66.06 km from the dam, 49.02 km from Peter's Lookout, 36.64 km from the lake head, of which we use 60 km - come from coordinates we chose ourselves. At the upper operating level of 532 m the summit sits 3,192 m above the water rather than 3,206 m, which moves nothing. And we could not retrieve the published survey description of the Badwater lake, so we quote none of it. A fair reader may see a retreat, since we concede the height dependence and then halve our own figure for it. The concession stands. The number is smaller because the earlier one came from ground nobody can photograph.

    Scope and sources

    Scope. This page answers Q4 (p.47) and pp.113-116. It does not answer the Chicago skyline (pp.110-111) or Lake Pontchartrain (p.112), which are long-path atmospheric cases belonging with the Rampion analysis, nor the pond experiment (pp.96-107), already answered on our bottom-up-observations page - which explicitly deferred these reflection pages to this one, and now closes that loop. Refraction bookkeeping follows the convention declared there: k around 0.13 for the standard atmosphere near the ground, k around 0.17 where that page and the Rampion page use it.

    Provenance. Ray-traced by us, scripts retained and publishable: every specular point, strip length and compression ratio, the cut-off identity, the glitter-path tilt budget, the Fresnel evaluations and the horizon distances. Quoted from sources we retrieved: the Aoraki height, the Pukaki elevation, the Cox-Munk relation, the Shaw-Churnside rules, the Sentinel-3 tilt, the GNSS-R method and the divergence-factor sentence. Withdrawn as unverified: the divergence-factor decibel values, and any description of the Badwater lake.
